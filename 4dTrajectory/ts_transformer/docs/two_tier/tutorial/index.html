Two-tier model tutorial

The two-tier model: a tutorial

A language model says what a controller would say. An autopilot flies only those words. This page explains the design and the code step by step. It uses real flights, and it has parts that you can change.

1 The idea

An air traffic controller does not fly the aircraft. The controller says short instructions: expect runway 23R, fly heading 270, descend and maintain 3,000 feet, reduce speed to 180 knots. The pilot, or the autopilot, flies these instructions.

The two-tier model copies this split into two parts.

  • The upper part is the prior. It is a language model of the controller. At each row, it says one row of words for each aircraft.
  • The lower part is the executor. It is an autopilot. It flies only these words with point-mass dynamics.
  • Between them is the vocabulary, the language of the words. A labeller reads words from observed tracks. These sentences train the prior.
  • The judge classifies what the executor flew: did it land, or not?
  • The post-training trains the prior again in a closed loop. The prior speaks, the executor flies, the judge decides, and a reward updates the prior.

Why two parts? A model that outputs positions can draw a smooth line that no aircraft can fly. A model that outputs words cannot do this. The executor flies every word with real dynamics. The model must also learn the real skill: when to turn, descend or go around, so that the aircraft reaches the runway.

The eight principles

Every part of the design follows eight principles. The outline of the design states them. Keep them in mind. They explain many choices later on.

1 · The words are the only interface

The prior gives no control values. The executor reads the words, the aircraft dynamics and the runway geometry. It reads no procedure data.

2 · The model decides the path

No executor law turns onto the final, follows the centreline or follows the glidepath. The model must say the words that do these things. A track that the procedure computes is not a skill of the model.

3 · Procedures are masks and judgements only

The procedure data (glidepath, decision altitude, final approach segment) blocks words during decoding and judges the flown track. It never makes a track.

4 · Airport facts are inputs, not identities

A word has the same meaning at all airports. Facts of the airport go into the inputs as measured values. No input is a code for the airport.

5 · One frame for the words of direction

Heading words are relative to the course of the runway in force. The same heading word means “downwind” or “final” at all airports.

6 · Each instruction has a target and an envelope

The labeller, the judge and the display use the same envelope code. A containment rate always comes with the width of its envelope.

7 · Inputs give only what a controller knows before the step

A labelled word is a target of the prior, never an input. The exception is the words said before the step.

8 · No compatibility

A changed word, spec or payload gets a new name. The code refuses an artefact of another name. There is no fallback for old files.

Three stages. Stage A builds the vocabulary, the labeller, the executor and the judge (sections 2–8). Stage B builds the prior (section 9). Stage C builds the post-training and the multi-aircraft work (section 10). The user sees each stage in the frontend before the next stage builds on it.

2 The vocabulary

The vocabulary is the language between the two parts. It has five columns. A row is one line of a sentence, and a row is Δ seconds long. In each row, a column says one word or says nothing. “Nothing” has its own value: unchanged.

#ColumnWhat a word saysClasses
0Runway“Expect this runway”: a candidate runway of the airport. Or “go-around”.candidates + 2
1Heading“Fly this track”: a track relative to the course of the runway, on a 5° grid.73
2Altitude“Descend or climb to this level”: a height above the airport, 40 levels. Or “no level-off”.42
3AngleHow steep: level, four descent classes, or climb.7
4Speed“Hold this ground speed”: a 5 m/s grid from 20 to 250 m/s. Or “unspecified”.49

Classes include the value “unchanged”. Source: vocabulary §3.1.

A speaker says the columns in this order. A later column sees the words that the earlier columns said in the same row. The first predicted step is the row 16 s after row 0. The rows before it are observed only. At the first predicted step, every column says a value.

The row interval Δ is 2, 4 or 8 s. The user chose Δ = 4 s (D11). A speaker says a row, and the executor hears it, every 4 s. The executor itself runs one cycle every 1 s.

The table above the figure shows the words of the rows that were said. Move the slider. At row 0 all five columns say a word. After that, most cells are empty. The heading column changes most often, because a turn is a series of 5° steps. Orange words are correction words. Section 7 explains them.

2.1 Heading words are relative to the runway

A heading word says: fly track θ and keep it, with θ = course(R) + 5k°. R is the runway in force and k is the class of the word. The heading classes go round the full circle. Class 0 is the course of the runway: the final. Class 36 is the opposite direction: the downwind. Classes 18 and 54 are the two base legs.

There are two reasons for a relative grid (D8). Both were measured on the train split.

  1. The final must be on the grid. The capture corridor allows 2° from the course. The courses of KSTL are 2.3° from the nearest absolute 5° value. With absolute words, no word could hold a KSTL final.
  2. The same manoeuvre gets the same word at all airports. This helps a model to use the data of one airport at another airport.

The executor converts a heading word to an absolute track when it hears the word. It keeps that track until the next heading word. A change of runway does not turn the aircraft.

2.2 Altitude words are heights above the airport

An altitude word says: descend or climb to level T and keep it. T is a geometric height above the airport elevation E (D58). The executor flies the level at T + E above sea level. The grid has 40 levels on three segments. The steps are 60 m up to 1,260 m, 120 m up to 2,700 m, and 450 m up to 5,400 m.

A coarse grid high up is enough: 99.7 % of the level words are under 3,000 m. A fine step high up would only divide the day-to-day spread of an assigned level. The word “no level-off” has no level. It means: descend at the angle in force, and do not stop. It is the word of the final descent.

Why above the airport and not above sea level? A word must mean the same manoeuvre at every airport (principle 4). At a high airport, such as KDEN, the levels of a final approach are far above what the five training airports (1–188 m) used. Above E, the final approach lies in the 60 m segment at every airport. The cost: a round level such as 3,000 ft MSL is a different word at each airport. The second part of the demo shows this.

The blue band is the tolerance ε of a level (D52): half the larger gap to the neighbouring levels, plus 10 m. It is 40 m, 70 m or 235 m. The envelope of a word is a tube around the descent to its level. Principle 6 requires that every containment rate comes with the width of the tube.

2.3 Angle words say how steep

The angle column has seven values: unchanged, level, four descent classes and one climb. The four descent classes come from a k-means fit on tan(angle) of the descent pieces of all train days. Each piece counts with the square of its length (D54). The user chose the values rounded to 0.25° (D56).

Descent 3 is the 3.0° glidepath that 24 of the 25 candidate runways publish. The vocabulary has one climb word. The word has no angle: the angle is a value of the executor. While a go-around is in force, the executor climbs at the steepest steady angle that its thrust permits. This angle is not less than 1.885° and not more than 3°.

2.4 Speed words are steps

A speed word says: hold ground speed V. Controllers assign indicated airspeed, but the data has no airspeed and no wind. So the words use ground speed. The word “unspecified” means that the pilot flies the approach speed of the aircraft type. In a labelled sentence, “unspecified” starts at the capture row.

A change of speed says each grid value on the way (D43). Each speed word is then a step of 5 m/s. The time between the words gives the rate of the change, in the same way that the time between heading words gives the rate of a turn. Why? A target word gives no rate. Take one target word and a fixed rate of the executor. Then, in 31 % of the flights, the flown aircraft moves more than 1 km ahead of the observed aircraft along the path (an estimate on the smoke artefact).

2.5 The runway column and the go-around

The runway column keeps two states: R, the runway in force, and G, the go-around state. The executor uses R for two things only: to convert heading words, and to find the distance for the slow-down to the approach speed. The judge uses R for the landing.

There is no clearance word and no runway lock. The labeller cannot see when a controller gave a clearance, because the data has no radio. So the model says the turns with heading words (D2). While G is false, the model can change the runway at any time (D12).

“Go-around” means abandon this approach (D10). It changes no target of another column (D27). The heading word in force stays. A climb word now climbs at the go-around angle, and “unspecified” holds the airspeed. The judge ignores every threshold crossing while G is true. The time limit of the flight grows by 900 s. A runway word ends G, so one column can say the sequence approach → go-around → approach.

3 The grammar

Not every row of five words is a good row. The grammar has six rules. They make sure that a row is consistent with the words in force and with the height of the aircraft.

  1. At the first predicted step, each column says a value. The runway column says a candidate.
  2. The runway column follows the table of section 2.5.
  3. When a row says an altitude word or an angle word, the level in force and the angle in force must agree at the height of the aircraft. A level that is more than its tolerance below the aircraft needs a descent class. A level that is more than its tolerance above needs the climb class.
  4. “No level-off” needs a descent class in force, or in the same row.
  5. “No level-off” is not permitted while G is true (D14). A runway word must end G first.
  6. A row that says “go-around” while “no level-off” is in force must also say a level above the aircraft (D27). Rule 3 then makes the same row say “climb”.

With rules 5 and 6, “no level-off” is never in force while G is true. Every climb of a go-around has a target from an altitude word.

One function, two uses. The labeller checks a row with the grammar. The speaker uses the same function as a mask when it decodes (D62). A speaker says a row column by column. A word of a column is permitted when some words of the later columns make the row pass. A check that sets the later columns to “unchanged” would refuse good words. For example, a level below the aircraft is grammatical when the angle column says a descent class. The function exists once, in instructions/grammar.py. A second copy in the prior would break the one definition.

Check it yourself. This demo is a JavaScript port of the Python grammar. The page was built with a test: 4,000 random rows and 120 random masks gave the same answers in Python and in JavaScript. The same test covers the heading reading and the lateral law of the next sections.

4 The labeller

The labeller reads one sentence from one observed flight. It works in two passes. The open-loop reading (this section) reads the observed words from the observed track. The closed-loop reading (section 7) flies these words with the executor and adds correction words. The sentences of the closed-loop reading are the training sentences.

4.1 Signals and the gate

The labeller reads signals from the data plane of the project: positions in the airport frame, geometric height above sea level, and rows of 2 s on even UTC seconds. It smooths the track (6 s), the altitude (10 s) and the ground speed (10 s). The test days are sealed: the labeller never opens them.

A gate refuses a flight, with a reason, when the flight cannot be said in the vocabulary. Examples: a landing runway that is not a candidate, a ground speed outside 15–350 m/s, a level outside the grid, or a go-around point on the runway. On the train days of the formal artefact, 44,338 flights were read, and 365 were refused. On the train and select days together, the most frequent reason was an impossible ground speed (166 flights).

4.2 The heading reading

At each row, the labeller takes the smoothed track L = 4 s later (the lead). The word is the grid value, relative to the course of R, that is nearest to that track. A new word is said when that track leaves the word in force by more than half a step (2.5°). So a continuous turn becomes one word for each 5°. The time between the words gives the turn rate.

Noise below half a step says nothing. This is a hysteresis: the track must move 2.5° away from the word in force before the labeller says a new word. The capture row is the first row from which the track stays in the capture corridor of the runway to the end of the approach. The corridor is 20 m + d·tan 0.45° wide, and the track must be within 2° of the course. The capture row uses later rows, so it is never an input of a model.

A flight is vectored when, before the capture row, its turns add up to 90° or more. Otherwise it is straight-in. On the train days, a straight-in sentence has about 5 heading words, and a vectored sentence has about 42 (open-loop reading at 2 s; readout.md of the artefact).

4.3 Altitude, angle and speed pieces

  • Pieces. The labeller fits straight pieces to the smoothed height against the distance flown. A piece grows while a least-squares line keeps every residual within 10 m.
  • Level. A piece is level when it lasts at least 20 s and every row stays within 25 m of its median. The word is the grid level nearest to the median.
  • Move. Every other piece is a move with a path angle. A run of moves says its altitude word at its first row: the next level, or “no level-off” for the last descent of an approach. Each move says its angle class.
  • Speed. The labeller fits pieces to the ground speed with the same method. A hold lasts at least 20 s within ±5 m/s of a grid value. A transition says each grid value on the way (section 2.4). “Unspecified” starts at the capture row, with two exceptions that keep an assigned speed longer.
  • Go-around. A low pass is a run of rows on the final of one candidate, at most 600 m high. It is a go-around when the aircraft held a level at least 150 m above the lowest point both before and after the pass. The runway column says “go-around” in the row of the climb word. The labeller reads each approach separately (D26): the words before a go-around must not tell a model that the approach will fail.

4.4 The row interval Δ

The data has a row every 2 s. The labeller reads each flight at 2 s. Then it puts the words on a grid of Δ seconds, so that one reading gives every Δ. Each word goes to the nearest Δ row (D45). A word exactly between two rows goes to the later row. When one row holds several words of a column, it says the last one.

Why the nearest row and not the next row? A word put on the next row is late by (Δ − 2)/2 on average. At 70–100 m/s, 3 s of lateness leaves 200–300 m of lateral offset after a turn of 90°. The ablation would then measure the rounding and not the row interval. The user compared the readings of the three intervals and chose Δ = 4 s (D11). A sentence at Δ = 4 s has about 89 rows. At Δ = 2 s it has about 179 rows (train, formal artefact).

5 The executor

The executor is an autopilot that flies the words with the point-mass dynamics of the project. It has no state that the words do not give and no law that makes a track from the runway or from a procedure (principles 1–3). It is a deliberate choice that the executor is simple. A parameter that was mined from data would mean that the executor does not generalise.

The executor readsIt uses it for

It does not read the threshold crossing height, the glidepath angle or the decision altitude. Only the judge reads them. A behaviour check proves this: if the executor flies the reference tracks again with every candidate’s vertical path changed and the frame’s origin moved, the states stay the same (D81).

5.1 One cycle: from wanted rates to controls

Each cycle lasts 1 s. The three laws below change the words in force into three wanted rates: the track rate χ̇*, the path-angle rate γ̇* and the airspeed rate V̇*. An exact inversion of the point-mass equations then gives the three controls: bank, load factor and thrust.

A = −χ̇*·V·cos γ / g        (= n·sin φ)
B =  γ̇*·V / g + cos γ       (= n·cos φ)
φ = atan2(A, B),   n = B / cos φ
T = m·(V̇* + g·sin γ) + D(V, n, h)

The limits act in a fixed order. (1) Bank: at most 32°, and changed at most 5°/s from the first cycle. (2) Load factor: between 0.5 and 2.0. (3) Stall floor: the speed law asks for no airspeed below 1.10 times the stall speed at that load factor. (4) Thrust: between −20 % and 100 % of the installed thrust. The drag polar is clean: no flaps, no landing gear and no speedbrakes. The negative part of the thrust range stands for these devices. A limited bank costs turn rate, never path angle.

5.2 The lateral law

When the executor hears a heading word, it sets the absolute target θ = course(R) + 5k° and keeps it until the next heading word. The word says the track L = 4 s later. So the executor turns to arrive on θ at that time. The track rate is the smallest of three rates:

  1. the error divided by the time left until the lead runs out (not less than two cycles; after that, the executor holds θ);
  2. the stopping rate √(2·g·p·|e| / V): the fastest rate whose bank the executor can still take out before the error is gone. Here p is the roll rate. The executor cannot know which word ends a turn;
  3. the turn-rate limit of 4.7°/s.

A new word measures its error from the target before it, so a series of words never reverses a turn while the aircraft lags behind. There is nothing else: no capture state, no turn onto the final and no centreline tracking.

5.3 The vertical law

The inner loop turns a reference path angle into a rate: γ̇* = sat((γ_ref − γ)/τ_γ, ±γ̇max), with τ_γ = 2 s. The modes choose γ_ref:

Words in forceModeReference γ_ref

A target that the executor has captured stays captured until a new altitude word or angle word arrives. So the mode does not change back and forth at the level.

5.4 The speed law

A speed word gives a ground speed. The executor flies it as the airspeed V_g / cos γ, but not below 1.10 times the stall speed. The speed changes at a_max = 1.4 m/s², and it goes exponentially into the last 5 m/s, so the change is monotone and does not pass the target. A speed word is a step of 5 m/s, so the executor makes each step in a few seconds. The rate of a longer change comes from the words.

The word “unspecified” is the published approach speed of the aircraft type. The executor scales it by √(m / maximum landing mass) and flies it as a true airspeed. It slows down to this speed at 0.25 m/s². If a larger rate is needed to reach it over the straight-line distance to the threshold, it uses that rate, but not more than a_max. While G is true, “unspecified” holds the airspeed that the aircraft had at the go-around row. Section 2.4 has the demo of the speed steps.

5.5 What the executor does not do

  • It has no runway lock (D12).
  • It has no capture state, no turn onto the final, no heading bend and no own intercept (D2).
  • It has no centreline tracking (D3).
  • It has no special law for “no level-off”: no aim at the threshold and no tube limit.
  • It has no glidepath floor and no level flight below the glidepath (D9).

The words do these things. Only the judge and the masks read the procedure.

5.6 The start, the three ways to fly, and the check

A closed loop starts at the first predicted step, from the observed state of that row. The start reads no sample after that row (D77). It takes the position and the height of the row. It takes the airspeed, the track and the path angle from the 2 s before the row (the rule displacement-2s, chosen by the user). The start bank is 0.

The code has three ways to fly: a single-aircraft batch, a multi-aircraft batch (each flight starts at its own cycle), and a single flight in plain floats. They must give the same states. A conformance check flies 250 labelled train flights in every way and requires the same states within 1e-6 m. It runs every time that a process opens an executor. There is no digest of code (D73). Section 8 explains this rule.

6 The judge

The judge classifies what the executor flew. It works on three layers. Layer 1 records, in each cycle, which limit bound. Layer 2 checks each word against the flown track, with the same envelope code as the labeller. Layer 3 decides the outcome of the flight.

An approach crossing is a crossing of the threshold plane of R with G false. The aircraft must be lined up: its track is within 30° of the course, and its offset is inside the landing screen. The judge gives the first event. At one row, it uses the order of this table.

OutcomeCondition

crossed_other_runwayG false: the plane of another candidate crossed, lined up, inside that runway’s own limit
timeoutNone of these within the time limit (the remaining observed time × 1.5, plus 900 s for each go-around)

The decision-altitude check (DA check) has no parameter (D38). At the DA point (the first row where the aircraft on the final descends through the DA), the height must be within ±22 m of the published glidepath. The lateral offset must be inside the FAS cone. The ±22 m value is the vertical bound of the evaluation module (ICAO Doc 9613). The cone is the lateral containment of an LPV approach (FAA Order 8260.58D). A “go-around” said before the DA point is not an event: the flight continues. So a go-around is a possible answer to an unstable approach. The reason is the regulation: the missed approach begins at the DA.

The judge does not grade quality (lateral, vertical and speed gates). That belongs to the evaluation module. The executor ends a flight where the judge ends it (D79): nothing is flown or said after the end.

7 The closed-loop reading

The open-loop reading gives the observed words. These words say directions and angles. They do not say positions. Real flights stay on their paths because of the guidance of the aircraft (LOC, LPV) and the radar of the controller. Their corrections are smaller than half a grid step, so the open-loop reading shows none: in the smoke artefact, the last heading word is class 0 in every sentence.

If the executor flies these words alone, the errors of the directions add up over 10–25 km. In a smoke test with an exact model of the words, 47 % of the vectored flights ended more than 106.7 m from the centreline. A finer grid does not help, because the real corrections are smaller than any grid step.

The closed-loop reading puts the missing corrections into words. The labeller flies its own sentence with the executor. When the flown path leaves the observed path by more than a tolerance, it adds a correction word. When the flown path is back, it says the observed word again (D32). A model that trains on these sentences reads the flown states. So it learns “an offset gives a correction word” from states that its own closed loop also gives.

7.1 The matched point: said where, not when

The labeller compares the flown state with the observed path at the matched point: the point of the observed path that is nearest to the flown position. The search goes forward from the matched point of the row before, so a path that crosses itself does not make a jump. There:

  • the lateral error e_y is the signed distance from the observed path, positive to the right;
  • the vertical error e_h is the flown height minus the observed height.

The reference is the matched point and not the observed point at the same time. A difference along the path is a difference of time. The comparison does not correct it. The observed words are said where the observed aircraft heard them (D42): at the Δ row nearest to the place where the matched point reaches the word. The controller gives a turn at a place, not at a time. An aircraft that is d ahead along the path before a turn of 90° is d to the side after it.

An overshoot (D53) is a change of sign while the error is still larger than the tolerance. The labeller then says the opposite correction in the same row. Waiting one row would add a lag that comes from the reading and not from Δ.

No correction is possible in some rows: a row that says a new observed word, a level hold, a climb, and the rows past the end of the observed path (D44). A correction is one class and needs time to work. A 100 m offset takes about 20 s to fix with one 5° class. So the flown path can stay outside the tolerance for some rows (D50).

The sketch shows the mechanism. During the turn, a new observed word arrives in every row, so no correction can start. The error grows while the executor turns in a different way from the pilot. After the turn, the correction starts. With corrections on, the error returns under the tolerance. With corrections off, it stays.

8 The artefact and its identity

The labeller writes the sentence artefact. It is a directory with the spec, the signals of each flight, the candidate runways, the sentences, and the closed-loop sentences for each split and each Δ. The formal artefact is instruction_language/v12_20261005 with the executor spec executor/v17_20261005. Both are read-only, with a checksum file.

Split of the daysDaysUse
train53Training

test14Sealed: never opened, never labelled, never put in a scene

The split is by operating day (a flight’s day is its landing day, UTC − 9 h), and the file of the split is committed. KAUS is a held-out test airport. Its data are not in the live training data.

8.1 What an identity binds

The rule of D21 is: an identity binds the format (what the words and the payloads mean) and the data rules (the sealed test days). Code is identified by what it does on fixed inputs, never by a hash of its source bytes. Data are identified by their flights, never by the bytes of a manifest.

  • The vocabulary: the spec sha. It is the format.
  • The labeller: a conformance check. The artefact holds a reference sample. The check labels it again with the code on disk and requires the same words, strata and refusals.
  • The executor: a conformance check on its reference tracks (section 5.6).
  • The closed-loop reading: its own conformance check, within the tolerance of the executor check.
  • The flights: the stored signals. A consumer that rebuilds a flight from the harvest compares it row by row.

Each check runs every time, in the process that uses the code. There is no passed record and no digest of code. Why? The earlier design named the passed records by a digest of the code. A change in a file that moved no track still refused every runner until the checks ran again. The user called a code fingerprint a bad design (D73). A code change that stays within the bounds opens everything, and nothing is retrained.

9 The prior

The prior is the upper part of the model: a language model of the controller. At each row, it says one row of five words for each aircraft, and the executor flies them. Stage B builds it. The code is on the branch dev-two-tier-v4-prior. This section explains its inputs, its network, its decoding with masks, and its training.

Status. The code and its tests are done. The formal campaign (cross-validation and the base model, milestone B5) waits for the review of stage A and stage B. So this page shows no result of a trained v4 prior. The demos of this section use made-up numbers where they say so.

9.1 What the prior sees

The rule is principle 7: an input gives only what a controller knows before the step. The rows before the first predicted step are observed. From the first predicted step on, every input that comes from a state comes from the flown states of the closed-loop sentence, as in the closed loop where the executor makes the states.

  • Own state. The height above the airport elevation, the ground speed, the vertical rate, and a flag no_motion that is 1 at row 0 only (D58, D60). No MSL height and no absolute position.
  • Candidate vectors. For each candidate runway: the aircraft in the frame of that runway (distance to the threshold, offset right of the final, height above the threshold, direction of motion minus the course), the height above its published glidepath (D13), and the landings on it in the last 30 minutes. There is no constant of the runway (D24).
  • Words in force. The runway in force as its candidate vector, the state G, the heading in force (sine and cosine of the angle relative to the course), the other columns as embeddings, and the time since each column said its word (D17).
  • Motion. From the displacement in the 2 s before the row, at every Δ (D25). Never the fitted track, ground speed or vertical rate of the signals: a fit over 15 s uses 7.5 s of the future.

Every input has a fixed physical scale in SI units (D41): distances as asinh(d / 1 km), heights over 1 km, the height above a glidepath as asinh(h / 100 m), speeds over 100 m/s. No input uses a mean or a spread of the training data, because a new airport has none.

No input is computed from R before R is said. The artefact writes the runway word at row 0, and its value is the runway on which the flight landed. An input of the rows before the first predicted step that uses it would give away the answer of the first predicted step (D23). A test checks it: a change of the runway word of a sentence leaves the inputs of these rows the same, bit for bit.

Evidence for principle 4: in an earlier prior, the replacement of the airport embedding with the mean of the four other embeddings raised the loss by 0.024 for each step and lowered the landed share in free generation by 5.8 points. The five embeddings were almost orthogonal: an identity table, not a property of the airports (readout R46). The v4 prior has no airport embedding.

9.2 Candidate tokens: any number of runways

Each candidate vector goes through one shared network and becomes a token. The tokens reach a row through one attention whose weights sum to 1. A sum would grow with the number of candidates. An attention does not. The runway head also points at the candidate tokens, so the model has no fixed number of runway slots (D41). A new airport can have 2 or 8 candidates.

9.3 The network

The network has no airport embedding, no row-position embedding and no input “time from row 0”. A row is the sum of: the projected own state, the time since each column’s word, G, the heading in force, an embedding for each other column’s word in force, the runway in force as its candidate token, and the attention over the candidate tokens.

  • Layers. Each layer is pre-normed and residual: a causal time attention, then a module that a caller can add (section 10), then a feed-forward network. A row reads the rows before it and itself.
  • RoPE. The query and the key of a row are rotated by an angle that grows with the time of the row in seconds. So the attention reads only time differences (D16). Seconds, not rows, so that every Δ reads the same time.
  • Cache. A speaker that adds one row at a time keeps each layer’s rotated keys and values, and encodes only the new rows. A key is rotated one time, when it is written.
  • Five heads in the order of the columns. Column k’s head reads the hidden state plus the embeddings of the words that the earlier columns chose in this row. The runway head scores each candidate token by q·token/√d, after “unchanged” and “go-around”. At the first predicted step, “unchanged” (and “go-around”) are impossible.

Configuration A of the design: d_model 192, 4 layers, 6 heads of width 32, feed-forward 768, dropout 0.1. It has 2,165,741 parameters (counted from the code). The RoPE base is 10,000.

9.4 Decoding: the speaker, the masks and the draw

The speaker says a row column by column. A word is said only where every mask permits it:

  1. the model’s own masks (“unchanged” at the first predicted step, a padded candidate);
  2. the grammar (section 3), with the look-ahead of D62;
  3. the procedure masks (section 9.5);
  4. the masks of a caller: a set of forbidden words for each column that the prior does not understand. Free generation gives one: “go-around” after the second go-around (D68).

The draw. The caller gives one uniform random number for each aircraft, row and column. The speaker takes the word where the cumulative probability, in the class order of the heads, first passes the number (D96). So the words of an aircraft depend only on its own numbers and inputs. This is what branch training needs (section 10.3). The speaker also records the words that all masks permitted, so that a later loss can score the same masked distribution that the speaker drew from.

9.5 The procedure masks

The prior speaks under three masks that come from the procedure of R (principle 3). The region is inside the FAF and the LPV cone of R. The join is the first row inside it.

  1. The glidepath lower edge, inside the region: the published glidepath − 60 m. A level T is blocked where T < edge − ε(T). “No level-off” is blocked where the aircraft is already more than ε below the edge. Outside the FAF, the floors of the RNAV procedures disagree with 10–14 % of the recorded tracks, so the edge acts only inside the region.
  2. The DA, wherever the aircraft is not inside the region. A level T is blocked below DA − ε(T).
  3. No climb, before the join, once the aircraft has been below the entry height (the glidepath at the FAF) by more than the band of the level nearest to it. No level above the aircraft’s height + ε, and no climb class.

A mask blocks a word when it is said, and never “unchanged” (D64). A word in force was permitted when it was said. A mask that made the speaker change it would fly the glidepath floor that the executor does not have. Whether to correct or to go around is the model’s decision, and the DA check judges it. While G is true, the “no climb” mask does not apply (D14).

9.6 Training and the choice of the design

Teacher forcing. The prior trains on the closed-loop sentences of the artefact. The loss is the cross-entropy of the five columns at every asked row (a row at or after the first predicted step). Each head reads the truth of the columns before it. The loss for each step is the sum over the asked rows and columns, divided by the asked rows.

Which sentences. The base learns only from sentences whose own words land (landed, D75). About 2.2–2.6 % of the sentences do not land when their own words are flown, mostly high at the DA. Their last words would teach an end that does not land. Flights with a faulty observed track are left out too (D111). The artefact is not changed: the selection happens when the sentences are read. The excluded flights come back in the post-training as starts of closed loops.

Stop. The training keeps the epoch with the smallest per-step loss on the select days. It never reads the validation days. They are read one time for each stage (D85).

Cross-validation by airport. The goal is a new airport. So a fold trains on the train days of four airports and reads the select days of the fifth. The campaign has 31 runs. The rules are fixed before the runs:

  • The score of a run is the mean, over the 5 folds, of the per-step loss on the held-out airport.
  • The seed scale is the absolute difference between two seeds of configuration A.
  • Configuration: among those within twice the seed scale of the best, take the one with the fewest parameters.
  • Variant: “constants” (with the length and the threshold elevation of each runway) only if it is lower than “full” by more than twice the seed scale.

9.7 A new airport

The prior has no identity of an airport. A new airport needs only data that the five airports also come from: the candidate runways with their threshold, course, TCH, glidepath angle and DA, and the airport elevation (from the FAA CIFP); the FAF and the LPV cone for the masks; the landings in the last 30 minutes (from the airport’s tracks or a live feed); and the dynamics of each aircraft type, which do not depend on the airport. Every fold of the cross-validation flies the full closed loop at its held-out airport. The final test is KAUS.

One limit is stated. Speed words are ground speeds. At a high airport, one indicated airspeed is a larger ground speed (about 8 % at E = 1,650 m, one step). The five airports and KAUS all lie below 200 m.

10 The post-training

The prior learned to imitate. But a good imitation is not the same as a skill: the prior must reach the runway with its own words. The post-training trains the prior again in a closed loop. The prior speaks, the executor flies, the judge decides, and the reward comes from the outcome.

Status. The parts C0–C7 and C9 are built on synthetic artefacts. The parts C8 and C10–C12 (the formal runs) wait for the base model of stage B and for the review of stage B. So this section shows no result of a post-trained model. The evidence it quotes comes from the earlier design (instruction-v3), and the text says so.

10.1 One stage, one commanded aircraft, a window of traffic

The closed loop is a window of recorded traffic with one commanded aircraft (D29). The model speaks for that aircraft. The other aircraft fly their records. A window starts at row 0 of the commanded aircraft. It ends where the commanded aircraft is done, or when it loses separation that it answers for (D93). The usual length is about 20 minutes, but it is not a bound.

The start model is the base model with a traffic attention whose output is zero. There is no single-aircraft stage, for three reasons. A window with no other aircraft is exactly single-aircraft free generation. A single-aircraft reward does not see when an aircraft lands, so it does not limit the spread of the landing times. And the model must learn to land one time, in the loop where it lands. On the train days, 60 % of the windows have no leader in the air at their first predicted step.

Windows. A round draws real windows and augmented windows in equal counts (D100). Window A inserts one aircraft that flies its record. Window D moves the aircraft ahead. Window B moves the start of the commanded aircraft: a turn about the airport of up to ±15°, a change of height of up to ±300 m and a scale of the speed of 1 ± 0.1 (D123). The four kinds are drawn from the same flights of a round, and a real window and its A, B or D go to different batches (D124). A window that opens inside a loss of separation is left out of the draw (D113).

10.2 The reward

The reward comes only from the outcome (D30). It is 1 for a landing without a go-around on a runway of the airport’s present landing direction. It is 0.9n for a landing after n go-arounds. It is 0 for every other outcome, and for every loss of separation. There is no payment for a go-around without a landing.

A go-around then pays when it turns a probable failure into a probable landing. With the same chance of landing before and after, it gives 0.9·p, which is less than p. A go-around that prevents a loss of separation keeps the chance of 0.9. So the same reward also teaches the go-around for traffic. “Go-around” is permitted at any row after the first predicted step (D10), and the chance p is low on many flights at the start. A payment would teach a go-around where the model is not sure, not where the approach is unstable. Before the training, the design measures whether the base model says “go-around” by itself on the final. If it does, the training needs no probes.

10.3 Branch training

One reward for a whole sentence gives every word the same advantage. This is poor credit assignment. Branch training (D37, D94) gives the advantage only to the words after a branch point.

  1. Each training window is spoken one time: the first sentence, with its own random numbers.
  2. If its reward is 1, the window gives no sample. A group whose rewards are all equal gives no gradient.
  3. If the reward is less than 1, find the event time t_E: the step of the loss of separation, of the judged outcome, or of the time limit. The branch points are the first predicted step and every 120 s after it, before t_E.
  4. A second pass speaks these windows again with the random numbers of their first sentences. At each branch point it copies the state K = 8 times: the executor’s loop, the speaker (cache and mask state), the separation judge and the window’s own state. Each copy continues with its own random numbers.
  5. A branch group is the first sentence and the 8 continuations of one branch point. They differ only after the branch point. The advantage of a sentence is its reward minus the mean of its group. It applies only to the words after the branch point.

Why this design? (1) Cost. In the earlier design, speaking took about 55 % of a round, and only 37–47 % of the sentences carried a gradient. Branch training spends the extra sentences only where the first sentence failed. A continuation flies only the part after its branch point. (2) Credit. Only the words after the branch point differ inside a group. (3) Branch points. A readout of the earlier design found that a new sentence from the start rescues the most events; from 120 s before the event, fewer; from 60 s or less, few. The branch points are fixed before the run, at the start and at fixed times.

A test in the code checks that a continuation with the random numbers of the first sentence says the same words and flies the same states, within 1e-6 m. The probabilities can differ in their last bits when the batch differs. A window that differs is counted, reported, and gives no sample in the round.

A second method: train on the landed sentences (D165). A measurement of the ceiling found that the model can say a sentence that lands in almost every window (99 % in at least one of 32 draws), but it gives that sentence too little probability. The clipped surrogate of branch training did not raise it further. So this method has no advantage and no clipped ratio. Each window is spoken several times by the model of the round. For each window, the sentence that landed with the highest reward is kept; a window with no landing gives nothing. The loss is the negative log-likelihood of the commanded aircraft’s words in the kept sentences, teacher-forced under its traffic, with the same data term and the same pull to the base model. Choosing the best sentence at speaking time is not wanted: only what the model is trained to say counts. Figure 12 draws the loop with branch training and Figure 17 the loop with this method.

10.4 The loss

The loss has three parts: the clipped-ratio surrogate (ε = 0.2) with the advantage of each branch group, the pull to the base model (weight 0.04, a KL estimate on the sampled words), and the teacher-forced data term (weight 1) on single-aircraft samples of the sentences in the selection landed. The traffic attention has its own learning rate.

  • The words are scored under the speaker’s records of the permitted words, so the loss reads the same masked distribution that the speaker drew from.
  • The ratio of a word is its probability under the model over its probability under a frozen copy of the model that spoke the sample. It is computed word by word.
  • Every counted row weighs the same: the sum over a batch is divided by the counted rows of the batch (D115).
  • Dropout. The surrogate and the pull run with dropout off, because the speaker spoke with dropout off. The data term runs with the dropout of the base’s training (D107). With dropout on, in a check on random weights, the ratio was off by 3 % on average and 22.5 % of the rows fell outside the clip band.

10.5 Traffic: tokens, attention and masks

The other aircraft of a window are every other flight of the airport and the split that is in the air at that step. They fly their stored signals. Their motion is the displacement in the 2 s before the row, and their R is the runway of their record from their own first predicted step on.

  • Edge features. For each other aircraft: where it is in the commanded aircraft’s frame (front, left, height difference); its place on the approach clock and whether the two can be compared; the relation of the two runways in force (same, single, dependent, independent, unrelated); the closing rate; the closest point of approach (time, horizontal distance, height difference); the distance that the rules require between the two; and a flag for an unknown motion. Every scale is a constant in SI units.
  • Traffic attention. At each layer, a module reads one token for each other aircraft: the edge features and the aircraft’s own motion (D98). Its output layer starts at zero, and its output is zero when a row has no other aircraft. So the start model equals the base, and a window with no traffic is free generation. One token network is shared by the layers (D116).
  • Established on the final. A function of one row: G false, the aircraft inside the region of the final of R, and its track within 20° of the course (D92). After “go-around” the aircraft is not established until a runway word ends G.
  • Separation judge. It uses the radar minima of FAA JO 7110.65BB with the rules for visual approaches. Parallel runways at least 2,500 ft (762 m) apart are free once both aircraft are turned in. An event of a window is the first loss of separation for which the commanded aircraft answers.
  • Speed-word mask. A mask of the caller on the speed column. It acts only when the aircraft and the one next ahead are both established, and while the aircraft is more than 5 NM from its threshold. It is a stated approximation: constant rate, no turn, no wind. It acts in a band that is only 0.1–2 km wide at most runways (D101).

There is no mask for the spacing on the final. The reward (0 for a loss of separation) teaches it. A mask is designed only if the readouts show that the losses at the join dominate.

10.6 What the earlier design measured

These numbers come from the multi-aircraft readouts of instruction-v3. They are evidence, not a result of the new design. A prior of that design, post-trained with one aircraft commanded, landed between 111 s earlier and 121 s later than its record on the select days (p10, p90). A loss of separation with the recorded traffic ended 11.5 % of its sentences. Most losses were on vectored approaches (21.6 %, against 4.0 % for straight-in). The selected round of its training had 1.75 points fewer losses on the validation days (11.1 % → 9.3 %). No readout has the base model of the new design in this loop.
