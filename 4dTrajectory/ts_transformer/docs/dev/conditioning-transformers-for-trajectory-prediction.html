Conditioning is the difference between a model that predicts what aircraft usually do and one that predicts what this aircraft will do, on this runway, in this wind, with that traffic ahead of it. Part A is the general toolbox, Part B is how the trajectory-prediction field uses it, Part C is your ATM problem.

Three ways to read this

  • Just tell me what to build. Cheat sheet below → §A.4 (adaLN-Zero) → §B.4 (goal conditioning) → §C.5 (staged plan). About twenty minutes.
  • I want the mechanisms. Read Part A end to end. Every mechanism has an equation, a figure, and code you can paste.
  • I want the field. Part B is a guided tour of the architectures that win benchmarks, Part C is what survives contact with aviation data.

A note on honesty before we start. The conditioning literature (Part A) is mature and well-replicated. The generic trajectory-prediction literature (Part B) is competitive and benchmarked. The aviation literature (Part C) is neither — results are reported on private datasets against each paper's own baselines. I flag this wherever it matters rather than quietly averaging over it.

PART AConditioning mechanisms

The general toolbox, independent of application. Six places a condition can enter a transformer, what each costs, and how to tell whether the model is actually listening.

A.1Where the condition enters

A transformer computes a function of a sequence of tokens. Conditioning means we also have side information \(c\) — a label, a scalar, a vector, a sequence, a set, a graph — and we want the output to depend on it. Formally we move from \(p(x)\) to \(p(x \mid c)\), or from \(f(x)\) to \(f(x; c)\).

What makes this tractable to learn is that there are only a handful of places a condition can physically enter a transformer block. Every published method is a variation on one of them.

The anchor result

The DiT paper (Peebles & Xie, Scalable Diffusion Models with Transformers, ICCV 2023, arXiv:2212.09748) directly compared four of these on identical models: in-context tokens, cross-attention, adaLN, and adaLN-Zero. adaLN-Zero won on both quality and cost. Per-block compute was 118.6 GFLOPs against 119.4 for in-context tokens and 137.6 for cross-attention, and at 400K training iterations its FID was roughly half the in-context model's. If your condition is low-dimensional, that is your default. Note the scope, though: this was established for class-conditional image generation, not for rich set-valued conditions.

A.2Input-level conditioning

Prepending condition tokens

The simplest idea in the whole field: turn \(c\) into one or more tokens and put them at the front of the sequence. Self-attention then lets every real token read them.

cond_tok = c_emb.unsqueeze(1)              # (B, 1, d)
seq      = torch.cat([cond_tok, x], dim=1)  # (B, T+1, d)
h        = transformer(seq)                 # every token can attend to cond_tok

DiT calls this in-context conditioning. It is trivial to implement and it handles both scalar and sequence conditions. It also costs extra sequence length — attention is quadratic — and it was the weakest of DiT's four options.

Adding to embeddings

For a condition attached to each position (a timestep, an agent-type label), just add: \(h = x + c_{\text{emb}}\). This is exactly the mechanism behind absolute positional encoding. Conditioning is the same operation with a different source for the vector.

Prompt tuning and prefix tuning

These are the parameter-efficient cousins of prepending. Prompt tuning (Lester, Al-Rfou & Constant, EMNLP 2021) prepends a handful of learned soft-prompt vectors to the input embeddings only, freezing the model; it becomes competitive with full fine-tuning at around 11B parameters. Prefix tuning (Li & Liang, ACL 2021) goes further and prepends trainable vectors to the keys and values at every layer:

$$K' = [\,P_K \,;\, XW_K\,], \qquad V' = [\,P_V \,;\, XW_V\,], \qquad \mathrm{Attn}(XW_Q,\, K',\, V')$$

Li & Liang tune roughly 0.1% of parameters while matching full fine-tuning on generation, and reparameterise the prefix through a small MLP for training stability.

The conceptual bridge worth carrying forward: a prefix is a learned, task-specific condition. If instead of learning \(P_K, P_V\) per task you generate them from a condition \(c\) through a small MLP, you have a general conditioning mechanism that reaches every layer. That trick is under-used in trajectory work.

A.3Cross-attention

Cross-attention is the canonical way to condition on a sequence or set \(c = (c_1,\dots,c_M)\). Queries come from the data stream; keys and values come from the condition:

$$\mathrm{CrossAttn}(x, c) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{(xW_Q)(cW_K)^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)(cW_V)$$ 
class CrossAttnBlock(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d, n_heads):
        super().__init__()
        self.norm_x = nn.LayerNorm(d)
        self.norm_c = nn.LayerNorm(d)
        self.attn   = nn.MultiheadAttention(d, n_heads, batch_first=True)

    def forward(self, x, c, c_pad_mask=None):
        # x: (B, T, d) data      c: (B, M, d) condition set
        # c_pad_mask: (B, M) True where padded -- essential for ragged neighbour sets
        q = self.norm_x(x)
        kv = self.norm_c(c)
        out, _ = self.attn(q, kv, kv, key_padding_mask=c_pad_mask, need_weights=False)
        return x + out

Latent cross-attention (Perceiver)

To tame quadratic cost over a huge input, the Perceiver (Jaegle et al., ICML 2021) introduces a small learned latent array \(z \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}\) with \(N \ll M\) that cross-attends to the large input, then self-attends among itself. Complexity drops from \(\mathcal{O}(M^2)\) to \(\mathcal{O}(NM)\). Perceiver IO adds a decoder cross-attention where an output query array reads the processed latents, producing arbitrarily structured outputs. Wayformer uses exactly this pattern to subsample large scene encodings.

The Stable Diffusion pattern

Latent Diffusion (Rombach et al., CVPR 2022) injects a text embedding into the denoiser via cross-attention at every block: spatial features query, CLIP tokens supply keys and values. Nothing about this is image-specific. Swap CLIP tokens for map polylines, neighbour tracks, or procedure waypoints and the mechanism is unchanged.

A.4FiLM, conditional LayerNorm, and adaLN-Zero

FiLM (Perez et al., AAAI 2018) is the foundational idea: modulate features with a per-channel affine transform whose parameters are predicted from the condition.

$$\mathrm{FiLM}(F \mid c) = \gamma(c) \odot F + \beta(c), \qquad [\gamma, \beta] = \mathrm{MLP}(c)$$

Two numbers per channel. It does not scale with resolution or sequence length, and it generalises conditional batch norm from style transfer and BigGAN. In transformers you apply the same idea to LayerNorm outputs, which is what adaptive LayerNorm means.

DiT's adaLN-Zero adds two refinements that matter more than they look. First, the modulation MLP produces six vectors per block — shift, scale, and a residual gate for each of the two sublayers. Second, that MLP is zero-initialised, so every gate starts at zero and the whole block starts as an identity function. Training then gradually opens the conditioning path instead of fighting through a randomly scrambled one.

def modulate(x, shift, scale):
    # x: (B, T, d)   shift, scale: (B, d)
    return x * (1 + scale.unsqueeze(1)) + shift.unsqueeze(1)


class AdaLNZeroBlock(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d, n_heads, mlp_ratio=4.0):
        super().__init__()
        # affine=False: the *condition* supplies the scale and shift, not the layer
        self.norm1 = nn.LayerNorm(d, elementwise_affine=False, eps=1e-6)
        self.attn  = nn.MultiheadAttention(d, n_heads, batch_first=True)
        self.norm2 = nn.LayerNorm(d, elementwise_affine=False, eps=1e-6)
        self.mlp   = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d, int(d * mlp_ratio)), nn.GELU(),
                                   nn.Linear(int(d * mlp_ratio), d))
        self.modulation = nn.Sequential(nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(d, 6 * d))
        nn.init.zeros_(self.modulation[-1].weight)   # <- the "Zero"
        nn.init.zeros_(self.modulation[-1].bias)

    def forward(self, x, c):                          # x: (B,T,d)   c: (B,d)
        s1, g1, a1, s2, g2, a2 = self.modulation(c).chunk(6, dim=-1)
        h = modulate(self.norm1(x), s1, g1)
        x = x + a1.unsqueeze(1) * self.attn(h, h, h, need_weights=False)[0]
        h = modulate(self.norm2(x), s2, g2)
        x = x + a2.unsqueeze(1) * self.mlp(h)
        return x
For your model

This is the mechanism I would reach for first. Runway identifier, wind vector, wake category, aircraft type, and distance-to-go all collapse to a single \(d\)-dimensional vector. Embed each, sum them, feed the sum to modulation. Adding a new scalar condition later costs one embedding table and no architectural change.

A.5Timestep and multi-condition fusion

Diffusion models must know which noise level they are denoising. The standard recipe is a sinusoidal embedding of the timestep through a small MLP, then into adaLN:

def timestep_embedding(t, dim, max_period=10000):
    half  = dim // 2
    freqs = torch.exp(-math.log(max_period) * torch.arange(half, device=t.device) / half)
    args  = t[:, None].float() * freqs[None]
    return torch.cat([torch.cos(args), torch.sin(args)], dim=-1)   # (B, dim)

# Fusing several conditions is just addition in embedding space:
c = (self.t_mlp(timestep_embedding(t, d))     # noise level
     + self.runway_emb(runway_id)             # categorical
     + self.type_emb(actype_id)               # categorical
     + self.wind_mlp(wind_uv)                 # continuous, 2-vector
     + self.togo_mlp(dist_to_threshold))      # continuous, scalar
# c -> AdaLNZeroBlock

Summing embeddings is not a hack — it is how DiT combines timestep and class, and it is what makes the mechanism extensible. Each condition gets its own encoder, and the block sees one vector. Keep the encoders small; the MLP after the sum does the mixing.

A.6Attention bias and masking

Rather than adding tokens, you can add a term to the attention logits. This is the natural home for relational conditions — relative geometry, map structure, who is allowed to influence whom.

$$\mathrm{Attn}(Q,K,V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}} + B\right)V$$

where \(B_{ij}\) encodes the relationship between elements \(i\) and \(j\).

  • Relative position bias (Shaw et al. 2018; T5's scalar variant) makes \(B_{ij}\) a learned function of \(i-j\).
  • ALiBi (Press, Smith & Lewis, ICLR 2022) uses a fixed linear penalty \(B_{ij} = -m\,|i-j|\) with a per-head slope \(m\). No learned parameters, a recency prior, and it extrapolates to sequences longer than those seen in training — train on short approach segments, run on long ones.
  • Graph and adjacency masks restrict which agents may interact. This is how the aviation literature encodes separation: both B-STAR and the BST-Transformer build the inter-aircraft interaction graph with a 10 km threshold and let each aircraft attend only within it.
def separation_bias(pos, r_km=10.0, soft=True, tau=4.0):
    """pos: (B, N, 2) aircraft positions in km, local ENU.  returns (B, N, N)."""
    d = torch.cdist(pos, pos)                       # pairwise great-circle approx in km
    if soft:
        bias = -d / tau                             # smooth decay, keeps gradients everywhere
        return bias.masked_fill(d > 3 * r_km, float('-inf'))
    return torch.zeros_like(d).masked_fill(d > r_km, float('-inf'))

# usage: logits = q @ k.transpose(-1, -2) / d ** 0.5 + separation_bias(pos).unsqueeze(1)
Watch the hard cut

A hard 10 km mask makes the loss discontinuous in aircraft position: a neighbour crossing the threshold flips from full influence to none. It works in the published models, but if training is unstable, the soft variant above is the first thing to try.

A.7Guidance: controlling how hard the model listens

These apply only to generative models — diffusion, mainly — and they control how strongly a sample obeys the condition. Uniquely among the six mechanisms, guidance is a sampling-time knob: you can turn it after training.

Classifier guidance (Dhariwal & Nichol, NeurIPS 2021) uses the gradient of a separately trained noise-robust classifier \(p(c \mid x_t)\) to push samples toward \(c\). It requires training that classifier, and it struggles with rich continuous conditions.

Classifier-free guidance (Ho & Salimans) replaced it almost entirely. Train one network to be both conditional and unconditional by dropping the condition at random, then extrapolate at sampling time:

$$\hat{\varepsilon}(x_t, c) = (1+w)\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, c) \;-\; w\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, \varnothing)$$ 
# --- training: drop the condition sometimes so the model learns both modes
if torch.rand(1).item() < p_uncond:          # p_uncond ~ 0.1 is typical
    c = self.null_cond.expand_as(c)
loss = F.mse_loss(model(x_t, t, c), noise)

# --- sampling: extrapolate away from the unconditional prediction
eps_c   = model(x_t, t, c)
eps_unc = model(x_t, t, null_cond)
eps     = (1 + w) * eps_c - w * eps_unc

Guidance generalises far beyond images. MotionDiffuser and the language-guided traffic simulator CTG++ both apply guidance — classifier-free or cost-function-based — to enforce constraints on generated trajectories. In an ATM setting you could condition on the assigned runway and sweep \(w\) to control how tightly generated approaches hug the nominal STAR, which is a genuinely useful dial for what-if analysis.

A.8Adapters, LoRA, hypernetworks

These condition by changing the weights rather than the activations. Adapters (Houlsby et al., ICML 2019) insert small bottleneck MLPs into each block and train only those. LoRA (Hu et al., ICLR 2022) adds a low-rank update to frozen weight matrices:

$$W' = W + \Delta W = W + BA, \qquad B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r},\; A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k},\; r \ll d$$

Only \(A\) and \(B\) train. Different adapters mean different conditions, swappable at inference with no change to the backbone. Hypernetworks take this to its conclusion: a small network generates the main network's weights — or its FiLM parameters, or its LoRA factors — from \(c\). Seen this way, FiLM is a hypernetwork that generates only affine parameters.

For your model

This is the clean answer to "one model or one per airport?". Pretrain a shared backbone on many airports in runway-relative coordinates, then attach a small per-airport LoRA. You get airport-specific behaviour at a few percent of the parameters, and a new airport costs a short fine-tune rather than a full training run. See §C.5.

How conditioning fails

The condition is ignored. The model learns to predict well on average while routing nothing through \(c\). This is the most common failure and the easiest to miss, because your loss curve looks fine. Detect it with a condition-shuffle test: permute the conditions across the batch at evaluation and see whether the metric moves.

@torch.no_grad()
def condition_shuffle_test(model, batch, metric):
    """If shuffling the condition barely hurts, the model is not using it."""
    y = batch['future']
    base = metric(model(batch['history'], batch['cond']), y)
    perm = torch.randperm(batch['cond'].size(0))
    shuf = metric(model(batch['history'], batch['cond'][perm]), y)
    return base, shuf, (shuf - base) / base       # want a large relative gap

Posterior collapse. In CVAE-style models the latent \(z\) is ignored because a strong decoder can model the data without it, and the KL term happily drives \(q(z \mid x,y)\) to the prior. Mitigations: KL annealing, free bits, a weaker decoder, or a discrete latent — Trajectron++ uses a 25-way categorical latent partly for this reason.

Over-conditioning and leakage. If you feed a ground-truth goal at training time, the model becomes trivially dependent on it and collapses when goals are estimated at test time. Always report results with predicted goals; reporting oracle-goal numbers alongside is informative, reporting them alone is misleading.

Guidance artefacts. Large \(w\) reduces diversity and produces physically implausible samples. Sweep it and report the accuracy–diversity trade-off rather than picking the value that flatters one metric.

Minimum verification set

Before believing any conditional model: (1) ablate each conditioning input separately; (2) run the condition-shuffle test; (3) sweep the guidance scale if generative; (4) report the oracle-goal versus predicted-goal gap if goal-conditioned; (5) look at attention maps. AgentFormer does this last one and shows attention concentrating on the target's own recent timesteps and on nearby agents — which is what you want to see.

PART BTrajectory prediction

Where the toolbox meets the problem. Trajectory forecasting is unusual in that conditioning is not a refinement — it is the entire modelling strategy.

B.1The problem, and why one output is not enough

Given observed history \(X = \{x_i^t\}\) for agents \(i = 1,\dots,N\) over past steps \(t = 1,\dots,T_{\text{obs}}\), predict futures \(Y = \{y_i^t\}\) over \(t = 1,\dots,T_{\text{pred}}\).

The naive approach outputs one trajectory and trains with L2. It fails for a structural reason, not a capacity reason: when the future is genuinely bimodal — this aircraft will be vectored onto either the left or the right downwind — the L2-optimal single prediction is the average of the two, which is a path no aircraft would ever fly. Mode averaging is the defining pathology of the field.

So the output is a distribution, and the families differ in how they represent it:

FamilyOutputTrained with
Mixture / GMM head\(K\) trajectories, each with a probability and per-step Gaussian Winner-takes-all: only the mode closest to ground truth is regressed, so modes specialise instead of averaging. Used by MTR and most Waymo entries.
CVAElatent \(z\) for intent, decode \(p(y \mid x, z)\) ELBO. Watch for posterior collapse (§A.9). Trajectron++, TrajAirNet.
Diffusiondenoise a trajectory from noise, conditioned on context Denoising loss. Most expressive, slowest — see LED's leapfrog fix.
Anchor / goal-conditionedendpoint distribution, then completion Two-stage. The most interpretable and, for aviation, the most natural (§B.4).

B.2What actually gets used as the condition

  • Past observed trajectory — always present, usually via a temporal encoder.
  • Agent type and kinematics — vehicle versus pedestrian; aircraft type, wake category, performance envelope.
  • Goal or endpoint — the strongest single lever on multimodality (§B.4).
  • Map or scene context — vectorised HD-map lanes for driving; runway geometry, STAR waypoints and the approach corridor for aviation.
  • Social context — the other agents, through interaction attention (§B.5).
  • Weather — marginal in driving, first-order in aviation.
  • Control or intent variables — turn signals, ATC clearances, or a desired return in the RL framing (§B.6).

B.3The architectures worth knowing

Read this section as a genealogy rather than a list. Three ideas recur: encode the scene in a relative frame, attend along agent and time axes separately, and decode with learned queries that each own a mode.

Interaction-first

  • AgentFormer (Yuan et al., ICCV 2021) flattens multi-agent trajectories across both time and agents into one sequence, then uses agent-aware attention that treats elements of the same agent differently from elements of other agents — preserving identity that naive flattening destroys. A CVAE latent supplies stochasticity. This is the closest generic ancestor of what you need.
  • Scene Transformer (Ngiam et al., ICLR 2022) uses a scene-centric representation with factorised attention alternating between the time and agent dimensions, and a masked-sequence formulation that lets one model do marginal, conditional, and goal-conditioned prediction. The factorisation is the template I recommend for multi-aircraft (§B.5).
  • Social-LSTM (Alahi et al., CVPR 2016) and Social-GAN (Gupta et al., CVPR 2018) are the origin of "social" modelling — pooling neighbour hidden states, and GAN-based multimodality with a variety loss. Historical, but the vocabulary comes from here.

Map- and goal-first

  • VectorNet (Gao et al., CVPR 2020) represents map and agents as polylines in a hierarchical graph — the move that made HD maps tractable for learning.
  • TNT (Zhao et al., CoRL 2020) and DenseTNT (Gu, Sun & Zhao, ICCV 2021) build on it. TNT predicts discrete endpoints then completes trajectories conditioned on them; DenseTNT drops the heuristic anchors and regresses a dense goal probability distribution. DenseTNT ranked first on Argoverse and won the 2021 Waymo Motion Prediction Challenge.

Query-based decoding — the current mainstream

  • Wayformer (Nayakanti et al.) is deliberately simple: a scene encoder with early, late, or hierarchical fusion plus latent-query attention for efficiency, then a cross-attention decoder where learned queries produce \(K\) trajectories. A good architecture to read first because it strips away everything optional.
  • MTR (Shi et al., NeurIPS 2022) and MTR++ (arXiv:2306.17770) introduce motion query pairs: a static intention query, obtained by k-means over ground-truth endpoints, handles global intention; a dynamic searching query refines locally across decoder layers. MTR-A won the 2022 Waymo challenge. Note what an intention query is — a learned goal anchor, i.e. goal conditioning wearing a transformer's clothes.
  • QCNet (Zhou et al., CVPR 2023) encodes every scene element in its own local relative spacetime frame, which makes the encoding roto-translation invariant, reusable across timesteps for streaming inference, and parallel across agents. Anchor-free proposals then anchor-based refinement. Ranked first on Argoverse 1 and 2.
  • mmTransformer (Liu et al., CVPR 2021) aggregates history, map, and social context onto fixed trajectory proposals, with a region-based training strategy to induce multimodality.

Generative

  • Trajectron++ (Salzmann et al., ECCV 2020) — the canonical CVAE baseline. Graph-structured recurrent model, discrete 25-way categorical latent for intent, GMM output pushed through a dynamics model so the outputs are dynamically feasible. Still the baseline to beat for probabilistic quality.
  • MID (Gu et al., CVPR 2022) brought diffusion to pedestrian trajectories, framing the reverse process as motion going from indeterminate to determinate.
  • LED (Mao et al., CVPR 2023) makes it practical: a trainable leapfrog initialiser skips most denoising steps, reporting speedups of 19.3× to 30.8× across NBA, NFL, SDD and ETH-UCY, with a 23.7% / 21.9% ADE/FDE improvement on NFL at the 3.2 s horizon. If you want diffusion in an operational system, start here.
  • MotionDiffuser (Jiang et al., CVPR 2023) does joint multi-agent diffusion with a permutation-invariant set formulation, a PCA-compressed trajectory representation, and constrained sampling for physical priors.

B.4Goal conditioning, and why it works so well

Future uncertainty is dominated by where, not how. Once the destination is fixed, the remaining path is close to unimodal and easy to regress. Goal-conditioned methods exploit this by factorising:

$$p(Y \mid X) \;=\; \sum_{g} \underbrace{p(g \mid X)}_{\text{a discrete, learnable choice}}\; \cdot\; \underbrace{p(Y \mid X, g)}_{\text{nearly unimodal}}$$ 
class GoalConditionedDecoder(nn.Module):
    """Stage 2. The goal enters as an adaLN condition, not as an extra token."""
    def __init__(self, d, n_layers, horizon):
        super().__init__()
        self.goal_mlp = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(3, d), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(d, d))
        self.blocks   = nn.ModuleList([AdaLNZeroBlock(d, 8) for _ in range(n_layers)])
        self.head     = nn.Linear(d, 3)
        self.queries  = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(horizon, d) * 0.02)

    def forward(self, ctx, goal):            # ctx: (B,d) history+scene   goal: (B,3) ENU xyz
        c = ctx + self.goal_mlp(goal)
        h = self.queries.unsqueeze(0).expand(goal.size(0), -1, -1)
        for blk in self.blocks:
            h = blk(h, c)
        return self.head(h)                  # (B, horizon, 3)
The evaluation trap

Training this with ground-truth goals is fine. Evaluating with them is not — an oracle goal makes almost any decoder look excellent. Report predicted-goal numbers as the headline. The gap between the two is itself a useful diagnostic: a large gap means your stage-one classifier, not your decoder, is the bottleneck.

B.5Multi-agent modelling

You have \(A\) agents over \(T\) timesteps, so the natural tensor is \((B, A, T, d)\). Attending over the flattened \(A \cdot T\) sequence costs \(\mathcal{O}((AT)^2)\) and scrambles agent identity. Factorised (axial) attention alternates instead: attend along time within each agent, then along agents within each timestep. Cost drops to \(\mathcal{O}(AT^2 + TA^2)\), and each pass has a clean interpretation — one is dynamics, the other is interaction.

def axial_block(x, time_attn, agent_attn, agent_bias=None):
    """x: (B, A, T, d) -- A agents, T timesteps."""
    B, A, T, d = x.shape
    # --- along time, independently per agent
    x = time_attn(x.reshape(B * A, T, d)).reshape(B, A, T, d)
    # --- along agents, independently per timestep
    x = x.transpose(1, 2).reshape(B * T, A, d)
    x = agent_attn(x, attn_bias=agent_bias)        # separation_bias() goes here
    return x.reshape(B, T, A, d).transpose(1, 2)

Coordinate frames

An under-appreciated decision that affects accuracy more than most architecture choices.

Whatever you choose, aircraft have no intrinsic order, so the model's output must not depend on how you index them. Relative pairwise encodings and set-based attention give you permutation-equivariance for free; anything that indexes agents absolutely does not.

B.6Return conditioning: a useful outsider

Decision Transformer (Chen et al., NeurIPS 2021) recasts reinforcement learning as sequence modelling: feed tokens of (return-to-go, state, action), and at test time condition on a desired return to generate actions that achieve it. Trajectory Transformer (Janner, Li & Levine) models whole trajectories as token sequences and plans with beam search.

The transferable lesson is not the RL. It is that a desired outcome can be prepended as a token, and the model will generate a trajectory consistent with it. Replace return-to-go with a target touchdown time, a runway assignment, or a required time of arrival at a metering fix, and you have a controllable ATM model — one you can query with "what would this approach look like if I needed it on the ground ninety seconds earlier?" That is a different and more operationally interesting product than a point forecast.

B.7Metrics, and what they hide

  • ADE — mean \(L_2\) error over all predicted steps.
  • FDE — \(L_2\) error at the final step.
  • minADE\(_K\) / minFDE\(_K\) — the minimum over \(K\) sampled trajectories. Standard for multimodal evaluation, usually \(K = 6\).
  • Miss rate — fraction of predictions whose FDE exceeds a threshold.
  • NLL — negative log-likelihood of the ground truth under the predicted distribution. The only one of these that rewards calibration.
$$\mathrm{minADE}_K = \min_{k \in \{1..K\}} \frac{1}{T_{\text{pred}}} \sum_{t=1}^{T_{\text{pred}}} \left\lVert \hat{y}^{\,k}_{t} - y_{t} \right\rVert_2$$
Four things minADE will not tell you
  • It scores the closest sample and ignores the other \(K-1\). A model can post a good number while producing \(K-1\) implausible trajectories.
  • It says nothing about calibration — whether the mode probabilities mean anything.
  • It rewards spreading samples out, which is easy to game.
  • It is a per-agent metric. It cannot see whether the joint prediction is scene-consistent, or whether your predicted aircraft fly through each other.

Always pair it with miss rate, NLL, and — for multi-agent work — a joint metric and a separation-violation rate.

C.2How aviation differs from cars and pedestrians

Borrowing architectures from the driving literature is right. Borrowing assumptions is not.

  • Strong procedural structure. Arrivals follow STARs and published approach procedures. Trajectories are far more constrained than pedestrian paths — but the constraint is a branching one, with discrete decision points (which runway, which downwind, extend or turn base). That is a gift to goal-conditioned models and a problem for regression.
  • Runway assignment is a dominant latent variable and shapes the whole approach.
  • Genuinely 4D. Latitude, longitude, altitude and time. The vertical profile has its own constraints and its own error budget; treating altitude as a third position channel with the same loss weight as horizontal position is a common and costly shortcut.
  • Control from outside the system. ATC vectoring injects behaviour that is invisible in the kinematics. You are frequently modelling \(p(Y \mid X)\) when the real process is \(p(Y \mid X, \text{clearance})\) and the clearance is unobserved.
  • Longer horizons, sparser interactions. Minutes rather than seconds, and interaction is governed by separation minima — typically about 3–5 NM laterally and 1000 ft vertically — rather than dense short-range social forces.
  • Weather is first-order. Wind shifts the whole trajectory; convective cells reroute it.

C.3The aviation literature

Multi-aircraft interaction — closest to your problem

  • B-STAR (Pang, Zhao, Hu, Yan & Liu, Knowledge-Based Systems 249:108998, 2022). A Bayesian spatio-temporal graph transformer, described by its authors as the first graph-based deep model for multi-aircraft interactive trajectory prediction. Spatial and temporal transformers interleaved in an encoder–decoder; the interaction graph is built from Haversine distance with a 10 km threshold; uncertainty is inferred from the data rather than preset. Evaluated on ETH/UCY and on real near-terminal ASDE-X data, with a separation-distance sensitivity study.
  • BST-Transformer (Li, Tian, Xie, Zhi & Wan, Aerospace 12(9):855, 2025). Terminal-area prediction with probabilistic conflict alerting. Two stages: an ST-Transformer combining temporal multi-head self-attention with a GCN over the same 10 km interaction graph, then a Bayesian variational decoder producing trajectory distributions. Monte Carlo sampling of that predictive distribution yields conflict probabilities against 0.6 and 0.8 alert thresholds. On Guangzhou TMA secondary-radar data the paper reports a 60.3% reduction in mean absolute displacement error against a deterministic ST-Transformer, with comparable reductions in the horizontal and vertical components. This is the single most relevant paper for your project; read it before you design anything.
  • Social ST-GCN (Xu et al., J. Aerospace Information Systems 20(6), 2023) — each aircraft a graph node, spatiotemporal graph convolution over time and interaction.
  • DA-STGCN (arXiv:2503.04823, 2025) — reconstructs the adjacency matrix through self-attention and graph attention, with probabilistic output; reports roughly 20% / 30% ADE / FDE reduction against prior 4D methods on self-built ADS-B data near BOS and JFK.

Conditioning and intent

  • Yin, Zhang et al., Neurocomputing 615:128843, 2025 — introduces a model-agnostic intention latent derived from local flight history that can be used as an additional condition by any trajectory prediction model. Robust to normalised inputs, improves generalisation to unseen areas, and is evaluated in both towered and non-towered terminal airspace. This is precisely the "add a condition" question you asked, answered inside your domain.
  • Context-aware Aircraft Trajectory Prediction with Diffusion Models (Yin et al., IEEE ITSC 2023) — trajectory encoder plus context encoder feeding a transformer-based diffusion decoder conditioned on pilot and controller intent and environmental conditions. Builds on MID.
  • Goal-oriented Diffusion Model for Flight Trajectory Prediction — two-stage goal estimation followed by a transformer diffusion model conditioned on the intention estimates, separating macro flight-pattern uncertainty from micro uncertainty. The aviation instantiation of §B.4.
  • Weather-conditioned Bayesian prediction (Pang, Zhao, Yan & Liu, Transportation Research Part C 130:103326, 2021) — the canonical weather-conditioning reference, using Seq2Seq plus CNN over convective weather products with Bayesian uncertainty quantification.

Sequence-modelling approaches

  • FlightBERT (Guo et al., 2022) and FlightBERT++ (AAAI 2024, arXiv:2305.01658) represent trajectory attributes as binary — later Gray — codes and predict by multi-binary classification. The ++ version is non-autoregressive and predicts differential values, reporting 29.0% and 21.8% mean-deviation-error reductions at horizons 1 and 15. Two caveats: it trains on a proprietary industrial ATC dataset, so it is not reproducible; and a single high-bit misclassification produces a large decimal error, which is a real failure mode rather than a theoretical one.
  • FlightPatchNet (arXiv:2405.16200) critiques that binary encoding directly and uses differential coding with multi-scale patch mixing for direct multi-step prediction.
  • Zhang, Payan & Mavris (AIAA Aviation 2024) on Zurich terminal data — a transformer matches an LSTM while training faster, and cluster-based prediction beats training on the whole set. Read that result carefully: the gain came from structure, not scale.

C.5Building this at your data scale

You have on the order of a thousand landing trajectories per airport, with room to download more. The honest answer to "should I use a large transformer?" is not yet, and the evidence is specific rather than a general appeal to caution.

Evidence on model size
  • On Zurich terminal data, a transformer merely matched an LSTM (Zhang, Payan & Mavris, AIAA 2024). The improvement came from clustering the data, not from the architecture.
  • Shan et al. (Scientific Reports 15:43100, 2025) swept configurations for HiFormer and found a moderate one — 64 CNN, 256 LSTM, 128 transformer, 4 heads — gave the lowest error at every horizon, while a higher-dimensional variant showed up to 49% higher RMSE at long horizons. Bigger was measurably worse.
  • A separate 2025 transformer study needed explicit noise injection to control overfitting.

Get the representation right first

  • Frame. Convert lat/lon/alt to a local ENU frame anchored at the runway threshold, rotated to the centreline. For multi-aircraft, layer a query-centric relative encoding (§B.5) on top.
  • Resampling. Fixed rate — 1 to 5 s; OpenSky studies commonly use 5 to 10 s. Interpolate gaps, filter with traffic, and clip to the approach segment rather than the whole flight.
  • Targets. Predict differences, not absolute positions. This is FlightPatchNet's critique of FlightBERT and it matters more than the encoding scheme.
  • Splitting. Split by time, not at random. Flights on the same day share weather, runway configuration and traffic; a random split leaks all three and will flatter your numbers by a wide margin.
  • Augmentation. Small rotations and translations within the corridor, noise injection, time warping. Then simply download more — a hundred airports at a thousand flights each is a different regime from one airport.

A staged plan

On transfer. Pretrain one backbone across many airports in runway-relative coordinates, then attach a per-airport LoRA (§A.8) or fine-tune briefly. MA-BERT (arXiv:2401.14421) demonstrates the pretrain-then- fine-tune pattern for ATM tasks. This is how a thousand trajectories per airport becomes a hundred thousand trajectories of shared structure plus a thousand of local idiosyncrasy — which is the only honest route to a model large enough to be interesting.

Red flags while training

Widening train/validation gap as you scale; the condition-shuffle test showing runway or wind being ignored; larger models degrading specifically at long horizons. Any of these means shrink the model and spend the effort on data and representation instead.

C.6Metrics that mean something to a controller

A single Euclidean ADE mixes together three errors with completely different operational consequences. Decompose it.

So the reporting set for an ATM model is: along-track, cross-track and vertical error separately; time error at a fixed point for true 4D; then minADE, minFDE, miss rate and NLL from §B.7; then a separation-violation rate for the multi-aircraft case; and the constant-velocity baseline beside all of it.

§Glossary

adaLN-Zero
Adaptive LayerNorm whose modulation network is zero-initialised, so each block begins as an identity map.
ADS-B
Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast. Aircraft broadcast their own position; the basis of OpenSky.
Along-track / cross-track error
Prediction error resolved parallel to and perpendicular to the intended path. Schedule error versus conformance error.
CFG
Classifier-free guidance. Trains one model to be both conditional and unconditional, then extrapolates between them at sampling time.
CVAE
Conditional variational autoencoder. Represents multimodality with a latent variable.
ENU
East–North–Up. A local Cartesian frame; here anchored at a runway threshold.
FiLM
Feature-wise Linear Modulation. Per-channel scale and shift predicted from the condition.
minADE\(_K\)
Average displacement error of the best of \(K\) sampled trajectories.
Mode-S EHS
Enhanced Surveillance. Carries airspeed, selected altitude and heading — useful for inferring wind.
Posterior collapse
A CVAE failure where the latent is ignored because the decoder models the data without it.
Query-centric
Encoding every scene element in its own local frame, with relative poses on the edges between them.
STAR
Standard Terminal Arrival Route. The published procedure linking en-route airspace to the approach.
TMA
Terminal Manoeuvring Area. The controlled airspace around a major airport where arrivals are sequenced.
Winner-takes-all loss
Regressing only the predicted mode closest to ground truth, so modes specialise rather than average.

§Caveats on the evidence

Read before citing anything above
  • Aviation numbers are not comparable across papers. There is no standard public 4D benchmark; most papers use self-built or proprietary data. B-STAR's results, the BST-Transformer's 60.3% MADE reduction, and DA-STGCN's 20% / 30% figures are each relative to that paper's own baselines. Treat them as directional.
  • Two specific figures I could not verify from primary tables: exact ADE/FDE values for B-STAR's aviation case, and for the 2025 Neurocomputing intentions paper. Both report beating baselines by a wide margin; confirm from the PDFs before quoting numbers.
  • The OpenSky receiver count often quoted elsewhere is not stated in the 2025 report, so I have given only the reply-count figure.
  • DiT's adaLN-Zero result is from class-conditional image generation. It is an excellent default for low-dimensional conditions. Its superiority over cross-attention for rich set-valued conditions — maps, many agents — has not been established in the trajectory domain, and I would not assume it.
  • Several 2025–2026 items are recent preprints and are neither peer-reviewed nor independently replicated.
  • The scale advice in §C.5 rests on two studies, not a broad meta-analysis. It points the same way as general small-data practice, but it is not settled science — and if you scale your dataset by two orders of magnitude, it stops applying.
