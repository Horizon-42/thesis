The LPV Final Approach Segment
— and what "LPV minimums" actually mean

A visual companion to optimization_constraint_design.md. Built on FAA Order 8260.58D, Chapter 3 (RNAV (GPS) Approach). Body in English; hover any underlined term for a bilingual (EN + 中文) definition.

1. What "LPV minimums" actually mean

An LPV approach is the most precise kind of RNAV (GPS) approach. Powered by WAAS/SBAS, it gives the pilot ILS-like guidance in both dimensions:

  • Laterally — an angular course that behaves like a localizer: it is wide far out and narrows as you approach the runway (§3).
  • Vertically — a published straight-line glidepath, normally 3.0° (§4).

Because both are guided, the pilot flies down to a DA — a single altitude at which they either see the runway and land, or go missed. That DA is the "LPV minimum". It is the headline number printed in the LPV row at the bottom of the approach chart.

📉 The number itself
Per 8260.58D §3-4-5: when the final OCS is clear of obstacles, the minimum HAT is 200 ft. So a clean LPV gives DA ≈ TDZE + 200 ft — essentially a Category-I ILS minimum, but with no ground equipment. If the OCS is penetrated, or the course is offset, the floor rises to 250 ft HAT.

The rest of this page builds up the geometry that produces that number — the FAS data block, the angular course, the glidepath, and the obstacle-clearance surfaces — and then maps each piece onto our trajectory-optimizer constraints.

2. The FAS data block — five numbers that define the final

Unlike LNAV (which is just a lateral box), an LPV final is fully defined by a small coded record called the FAS data block (8260.58D §3-1-1.f). Five fields drive everything:

FieldWhat it isHow it is fixed (8260.58D)
LTP/FTPThe runway threshold reference point. Its elevation is the TDZE; it is the origin everything is measured from.Surveyed runway threshold.
FPAPSets the direction of the lateral course.From LTP toward the DER, a distance = greater of (runway length, 9023 ft).
Length offsetWhere lateral sensitivity switches to missed-approach sensitivity.DER-to-FPAP distance (0 for offset procedures).
Course widthThe lateral full-scale half-width at the threshold.max(350 ft, tan(1.5°)·dGARP) — typically ≈ 108 m / 350 ft (§3).
GPA & TCHThe glidepath angle and its height over the threshold.GPA normally 3.0° (max 3.5°); TCH ≤ 60 ft (§4).

One derived point matters a lot: the GARP, located 1000 ft beyond the FPAP. It is the apex from which the angular lateral guidance fans out.

3. Lateral geometry — an angular course, not a box

This is the single biggest difference from LNAV. The lateral guidance is a sector that originates at the GARP and opens out toward the PFAF. Its angular half-width is the LGSA, between ±1.5° and ±2°. Converted to a distance, the edge of full-scale deflection (a CDI reading of ±1.0) is:

half-width(P) = course_width · dGARP(P) / dGARP(LTP) course_width = greater of 350 ft or tan(1.5°) · dGARP(LTP) // 8260.58D Formula 3-1-1

So the corridor is widest at the PFAF and squeezes down to ±350 ft (≈108 m) at the threshold — exactly like an ILS localizer. Compare that to LNAV, whose protected area is a constant ±0.6 NM box. (Note: ±350 ft is the guidance full-scale; the obstacle-evaluation surfaces in §5 are wider.)

4. Vertical geometry — the published glidepath

The glidepath is treated as a straight line in space (8260.58D §3-4-4.d): it leaves the threshold at height TCH above the LTP and climbs back along the course at the GPA. For a straight-in approach the path altitude at horizontal distance d back from the LTP is, to first order:

hGP(d) = TDZE + TCH + d · tan(GPA) // straight-in; LTP elevation = TDZE

Key LPV-specific limits (8260.58D §3-4-1, §1-3-1.f):

  • GPA normally 3.0°; maximum 3.5° (only for a HAT below 250 ft).
  • TCH rounded to the nearest foot, maximum 60 ft; the default gives a 30 ft WCH.
  • The point where the glidepath meets the runway is the GPIP, at TCH / tan(GPA) beyond the LTP (e.g. 55 ft @ 3.1° ≈ 1018 ft).

5. Obstacle Clearance Surfaces — W, X, Y

The DA is not just "200 ft" by decree — it is the lowest altitude at which the protected airspace under the glidepath is still obstacle-free. That protected airspace is built from three surfaces (8260.58D §3-4-4):

  • W surface — the primary surface. It slopes along the track and is level perpendicular to it. Its slope is 102/θ (≈ 34:1 for a 3° GPA), i.e. an OCS angle of only atan(θ/102) ≈ 1.74°. Half-width grows from 400 ft at the origin to 2200 ft far out: W = 0.036·dLTP + 392.8 ft.
  • X surface — slopes upward from the edge of the W surface, perpendicular to track (secondary protection).
  • Y surface — an early missed-approach transitional surface outboard of X.

An obstacle in X or Y is first raised to its effective elevation and then evaluated against the W surface. If anything pokes through, the DA is raised (or the GPA steepened / TCH adjusted) until clearance is restored.

6. The Decision Altitude = the minimum (putting it together)

The published DA is the highest of three candidate altitudes (8260.58D §3-4-5):

  1. the DA from the applicable minimum HAT — 200 ft if the OCS is clear, 250 ft if the OCS is penetrated or the course is offset;
  2. the DA required by the GPA (steeper paths need more height — per 8260.3 table 3-2-2);
  3. the DA from the OCS evaluation — pushed up by any obstacle penetration of the W/X/Y surfaces (§5).
🎯 In one sentence
The "LPV minimum" is the DA: fly the angular course and the 3° glidepath down to ≈ TDZE + 200 ft (best case), and at that altitude either land or go missed. Everything in §2–§5 exists to justify that one number.
