9 The prior

The prior is the upper part of the model: a language model of the controller. At each row, it says one row of five words for each aircraft, and the executor flies them. Stage B builds it. The code is on the branch dev-two-tier-v4-prior. This section explains its inputs, its network, its decoding with masks, and its training.

Status. The code and its tests are done. The formal campaign (cross-validation and the base model, milestone B5) waits for the review of stage A and stage B. So this page shows no result of a trained v4 prior. The demos of this section use made-up numbers where they say so.

9.1 What the prior sees

The rule is principle 7: an input gives only what a controller knows before the step. The rows before the first predicted step are observed. From the first predicted step on, every input that comes from a state comes from the flown states of the closed-loop sentence, as in the closed loop where the executor makes the states.

  • Own state. Four numbers about the aircraft itself (D58, D60). They give no position and no direction. Where the aircraft is, and where it flies, is in the candidate vectors (next item).
    NumberWhat it isUnit and scale
    HeightHow high the aircraft is above the airport elevation E. It is not the height above the sea (MSL): with the height above the sea, the elevation of the airport would be a constant that names the airport (D24). The altitude words also use the height above E.m; height / 1 km
    Ground speedHow fast the aircraft moves over the ground, in the horizontal plane only. It is not the airspeed: the data has no wind.m/s; speed / 100 m/s
    Vertical rateHow fast the height changes: the change of the height in one second. It is positive in a climb and negative in a descent. Example: on a 3° glidepath at a ground speed of 70 m/s, the aircraft goes down about 3.7 m every second, so the vertical rate is about −3.7 m/s. In level flight it is 0.m/s; rate / 10 m/s
    no_motionA flag: 1 at row 0 of an aircraft, 0 at every other row. At row 0 there is no stored state 2 s before, so the ground speed and the vertical rate cannot be measured there and are set to 0. The flag tells the network that these zeros mean “not known”, and not “standing still”.0 or 1
    The ground speed and the vertical rate are measured from the past only (D25): the distance that the aircraft moved in the 2 s before the row, over 2 s. The horizontal part of the move gives the ground speed. The change of the height gives the vertical rate. A fitted or stored velocity is not used: such a fit reads rows after the row, and an input must not know the future.
  • Candidate vectors. The prior does not know where the aircraft is on a map. For each candidate runway (a runway end that has a published vertical path), it gets one short vector that says where the aircraft is as seen from that runway. An airport with 5 candidates gives 5 vectors at each row (D5, D23). A vector has six kinds of number:
    NumberWhat it isScale
    Distance before the thresholdHow far the aircraft is before the runway threshold, measured along the course of the runway (the direction of landing).asinh(d / 1 km)
    Offset right of the finalHow far the aircraft is to the right of the extended centreline of the runway. A negative value is left of it.asinh(d / 1 km)
    Height above the thresholdHow high the aircraft is above the threshold of this runway.h / 1 km
    Direction of motion minus the courseThe angle between the direction in which the aircraft moves and the direction of landing. It is given as a sine and a cosine, so that 359° and 1° are close to each other. A value of 0° is “flying to the runway”, and 180° is “flying away from it”.sine, cosine
    Height above the glidepathHow far the aircraft is above (positive) or below (negative) the published glidepath of this runway at its present distance (D13). The value is useful near the final only; the offset tells the model if it is near.asinh(h / 100 m)
    Landings in the last 30 minutesHow many aircraft landed on this runway in the 30 minutes before the row. It shows which runways are in use now. A flight never counts its own landing.n / 10
    Example. A KRDU arrival is on the left downwind of runway 23R (course 225°). It is 3 km before the threshold and 5 km left of the final, and it flies on track 045°. In the vector of 23R: distance 3 km, offset −5 km, direction of motion minus the course 180° (sine 0, cosine −1), so the model sees “parallel to the runway, going the other way”. In the vector of the parallel runway 23L, the numbers are different (another offset), and in the vector of the opposite runway 05L the distance is negative (the aircraft is past that runway) and it flies in that runway’s landing direction. So the same aircraft has a different vector for each candidate, and the model compares them to decide which runway to expect.
    There is no constant of the runway in a vector: no length, no elevation, no name (D24). Such a constant would identify the runway, and with it the airport, and the model would learn the airport and not the geometry. The vector has only numbers that change with the aircraft.
  • Words in force. What the prior has said so far, and is still holding: the runway in force as its candidate vector, the state G (go-around or not), the heading in force (sine and cosine of the angle relative to the course), and the altitude, angle and speed words as embeddings. A word stays in force until the column says a new one. Before the first predicted step, nothing has been said, and every column is “none yet”.
    Time since each column said its word (D17). Each of the five columns also gets one number: how many seconds ago this column last said a word. Why the model needs it: a row in which a column says “unchanged” does not tell how old the word in force is. The age matters for the next word.
    • A heading word said 4 s ago means that the aircraft is probably still turning. A heading word said 120 s ago means that the aircraft has long been on that track.
    • An altitude word said just now means that the aircraft is still on its way to the level. After a long time, it has arrived and may need the next word.
    • The speed and the angle words work the same way. And because the executor makes each change at a fixed rate, the time since a word tells how far the change has gone.
    The number is in seconds for every column, so that every row interval Δ reads the same time. It is compressed with a logarithm: log(1 + t / 2 s) / 5. Small times then differ clearly (4 s gives 0.22), and long times do not grow without bound (120 s gives 0.82). It is 0 up to and with the first predicted step, because no column has said anything yet. For the runway column, it is the time since the runway was given, or given again (a change of runway, or the runway word that ends a go-around). The word “go-around” does not restart it, because a go-around keeps the runway in force.
  • Motion. From the displacement in the 2 s before the row, at every Δ (D25). Never the fitted track, ground speed or vertical rate of the signals: a fit over 15 s uses 7.5 s of the future.

Every input has a fixed physical scale in SI units (D41): distances as asinh(d / 1 km), heights over 1 km, the height above a glidepath as asinh(h / 100 m), speeds over 100 m/s. No input uses a mean or a spread of the training data, because a new airport has none.

No input is computed from R before R is said. The artefact writes the runway word at row 0, and its value is the runway on which the flight landed. An input of the rows before the first predicted step that uses it would give away the answer of the first predicted step (D23). A test checks it: a change of the runway word of a sentence leaves the inputs of these rows the same, bit for bit.

Evidence for principle 4: in an earlier prior, the replacement of the airport embedding with the mean of the four other embeddings raised the loss by 0.024 for each step and lowered the landed share in free generation by 5.8 points. The five embeddings were almost orthogonal: an identity table, not a property of the airports (readout R46). The v4 prior has no airport embedding.

9.2 Candidate tokens: any number of runways

Each candidate vector goes through one shared network (a Linear layer, a GELU, and a second Linear layer, both d wide) and becomes a token. The tokens reach a row through one attention whose weights sum to 1. A sum would grow with the number of candidates. An attention does not. The runway head also points at the candidate tokens, so the model has no fixed number of runway slots (D41). A new airport can have 2 or 8 candidates.

9.3 The network

The network is a small Transformer, in the same family as the language models that say one word after another. It has three parts, and Figure 18 shows them:

  1. The input of a row. All the inputs of 9.1 and 9.2 become one vector of d numbers for each row.
  2. Four layers. Each layer lets a row read the rows before it, and then lets it read the other aircraft. The result is the hidden state h: what the network knows about the situation at this row.
  3. Five heads. Each head turns the hidden state into the probabilities of the words of one column. The heads speak in the order of the columns.

The network has no airport embedding, no row-position embedding and no input “time from row 0”. Which airport it is, and how late in the flight it is, must follow from the physical inputs.

Step 1: the input of a row (Figure 18a)

Four pieces are added together. Each piece first becomes a vector of d numbers.

  • A Linear layer on 12 numbers: the own state (4 numbers, 9.1), the time since each of the 5 columns said its word (5), G (1) and the heading in force as sine and cosine (2). A Linear layer multiplies the numbers by a table of weights, and the training chooses the weights.
  • Three embeddings: one for each of the altitude, the angle and the speed word in force. An embedding is a table with one learned vector for each word. Index 0 is “none yet”, the value before the first predicted step.
  • The runway in force: the token of that candidate (9.2) through a Linear layer. If no runway has been said yet, one learned vector stands in its place.
  • The candidate pool (9.2). This is one attention over the candidate tokens. It lets the row read the runway information that matters now, in a vector of fixed width, whatever the number of candidates. It works in four steps:
    1. The query is the row built so far (the sum of the three pieces above). It asks: “In my situation, which candidate runways matter to me?”
    2. Each candidate token gives a key (a label) and a value (a content). The query is compared with every key, and each comparison gives a score.
    3. The scores go through a softmax. The result is one weight for each candidate. The weights are not negative, and they sum to 1. A padded slot, which is not a real candidate, gets the weight 0.
    4. The result is the weighted average of the values, through one more Linear layer. It is added to the row.
    Why attention, and not a sum or a list. A sum of the tokens would grow with the number of candidates, and a list would need a fixed number of slots. The weights always sum to 1, so the result has the same size at an airport with 4 candidates and at one with 8. If the candidates are put in another order, the result does not change, so the network cannot use “the third slot” as a signal. This is a single attention head with its own weights. It is different from the causal time attention in step 2: that one looks back in time over the rows, and this one looks across the runways of one row. The choice of the runway word is not made here: the runway head scores the same tokens again (step 3).

The sum is the vector x that enters layer 1. Where each of the pieces comes from was fixed in 9.1: the network gets only physical numbers.

Step 2: one layer (Figure 18b)

The same layer is used four times. A layer is pre-normed and residual. This means: the vector of the row, x, runs through the layer like a rail. Each block of the layer reads a normalised copy of x, and adds its result back to x. The block cannot erase x, it can only add to it. A layer has three blocks:

  1. Causal time attention. The row asks a question (a query), each earlier row has a label (a key) and a content (a value). The row takes the contents of the rows whose labels match its question best. “Causal” means that a row reads the rows before it and itself, and never a later row. The row always reads itself, so no row has all its keys blocked.
    The network has to know how long ago a row was. RoPE does it. Before the query and the key are compared, each is rotated by an angle that grows with the time of the row in seconds (D16). Then the comparison depends only on the difference of two times, and never on the time itself. Seconds, not rows, so that every row interval Δ reads the same time. The attention has several heads, each 32 numbers wide, and each head can look for a different thing.
  2. The added module (the traffic attention, section 10). It reads the other aircraft, and its result is added to x without a normalisation. In the base prior alone, this block is empty. The post-training adds it (its output starts at zero).
  3. Feed-forward. A small network that works on each row alone: a Linear layer that makes the vector 4 times wider, a GELU (a smooth switch that lets a number pass, or not), and a Linear layer back to d.

After the four layers, one more normalisation gives the hidden state h. Dropout (a random 10 % of the numbers set to 0 during training, so that the network does not rely on one number) is in the attention, after the attention block, inside the feed-forward and after it. It is off when the prior speaks.

Cache. A speaker adds one row at a time. It keeps the keys and values of every layer, already rotated, and encodes only the new row. A key is rotated one time, when it is written. The result is the same as encoding all the rows again, up to the rounding of the sums.

Step 3: the five heads (Figure 18c)

The heads speak in the order of the columns: runway, heading, altitude, angle, speed. Head k does not read h alone. It reads gk = h + the embeddings of the words that the earlier columns said in this same row. So the heading head knows the runway word of this row, and the speed head knows all four words before it. The model is thus autoregressive over the columns, and the rows before it give the second direction (Figure 8). In training the earlier words are the true words (teacher forcing). When the prior speaks, they are the words it has drawn.

  • A word head is one Linear layer from gk to the classes of its column: 73 for the heading, 42 for the altitude, 7 for the angle and 49 for the speed (each includes “unchanged”).
  • The runway head has two fixed classes, “unchanged” and “go-around”, and one score for each candidate. The score of a candidate is q · token / √d, where q is a learned view of g1 and token is the candidate token of 9.2. So the head points at a candidate, and the network has no fixed number of runway slots. A padded slot gets −∞.
  • At the first predicted step, the heads must say a value: “unchanged” is impossible in every column, and so is “go-around” in the runway column.

The sizes

Every attention head is 32 numbers wide, so d = 32 × the number of heads. The design starts from configuration A (d 192, 4 layers, 6 heads, feed-forward 768, dropout 0.1). The cross-validation by airport chose configuration C (d 256, 4 layers, 8 heads, feed-forward 1,024) for the base model. The RoPE base is 10,000: the periods of the rotations run from 6.3 s to about 35,000 s, which is ten times the longest sentence with a go-around. The parameters, counted from the code:

9.4 Decoding: the speaker, the masks and the draw

The speaker says a row column by column. A word is said only where every mask permits it:

  1. the model’s own masks (“unchanged” at the first predicted step, a padded candidate);
  2. the grammar (section 3), with the look-ahead of D62;
  3. the procedure masks (section 9.5);
  4. the masks of a caller: a set of forbidden words for each column that the prior does not understand. Free generation gives one: “go-around” after the second go-around (D68).

The draw. The caller gives one uniform random number for each aircraft, row and column. The speaker takes the word where the cumulative probability, in the class order of the heads, first passes the number (D96). So the words of an aircraft depend only on its own numbers and inputs. This is what branch training needs (section 10.3). The speaker also records the words that all masks permitted, so that a later loss can score the same masked distribution that the speaker drew from.

Why a draw, and not the most probable word

The prior does not say a word. For each column of each row it gives probabilities, for example 55 % for “unchanged” and 25 % for 70 m/s. The speaker could take the word with the highest probability in each column. It does not. It draws a word by chance, with its probability. This is the usual way to use a language model and a policy in reinforcement learning, and in this design it is a decision: the speaker draws from the model’s own distribution, at a temperature of 1 (D121).

How a draw works. The speaker needs a random number u, a number between 0 and 1. It lays the words of the column on a line from 0 to 1, and gives each word a piece as long as its probability. The word whose piece holds u is said (Figure 21).

  • The same number, the same situation, the same word. If the probabilities are the same, the same u gives the same word.
  • The same number, a different situation, perhaps another word. If the inputs change (for example another aircraft moved differently), the probabilities change, the pieces move, and the same u can fall in another word.

Why not the most probable word.

  1. The most probable word is “unchanged” in most rows. A row lasts 4 s, and in most rows nothing happens: “unchanged” has the largest probability, and each new word (a turn, a descent, a go-around) has a smaller one. If the speaker always took the largest, no aircraft would ever start a turn or a descent. Drawing makes these events happen at about the rate that the data has. This is my reading of the mechanism. The project has no run that takes the most probable word, so it is not measured.
  2. The training needs sentences that differ. The post-training compares the rewards of different sentences of one window (section 10.3). If every sentence were the same, all the rewards would be the same, the advantages would be 0, and nothing could be learnt. The draw makes the sentences differ. The ceiling readout shows how much: for the same model and the same windows, one draw lands in 79.5 % to 85.7 % of the windows, and 32 draws land at least once in 99.3 % of them.
  3. A probability says that several instructions are reasonable. In the same situation, controllers say different things. The largest probability alone would hide that.

Who gives the numbers. When the prior is used for real, the numbers are simply random. In the training and in the readouts, they are the caller’s and are fixed on purpose, so that a run can be repeated and two runs can be compared (the selection readout speaks the same windows with the same numbers every round). In the post-training, each aircraft has a stream of numbers of its own (section 10.7).

9.5 The procedure masks

The prior speaks under three masks that come from the procedure of R (principle 3). The region is inside the FAF and the LPV cone of R. The join is the first row inside it.

  1. The glidepath lower edge, inside the region: the published glidepath − 60 m. A level T is blocked where T < edge − ε(T). “No level-off” is blocked where the aircraft is already more than ε below the edge. Outside the FAF, the floors of the RNAV procedures disagree with 10–14 % of the recorded tracks, so the edge acts only inside the region.
  2. The DA, wherever the aircraft is not inside the region. A level T is blocked below DA − ε(T).
  3. No climb, before the join, once the aircraft has been below the entry height (the glidepath at the FAF) by more than the band of the level nearest to it. No level above the aircraft’s height + ε, and no climb class.

A mask blocks a word when it is said, and never “unchanged” (D64). A word in force was permitted when it was said. A mask that made the speaker change it would fly the glidepath floor that the executor does not have. Whether to correct or to go around is the model’s decision, and the DA check judges it. While G is true, the “no climb” mask does not apply (D14).

9.6 Training and the choice of the design

Teacher forcing. The prior trains on the closed-loop sentences of the artefact. The loss is the cross-entropy of the five columns at every asked row (a row at or after the first predicted step). Each head reads the truth of the columns before it. The loss for each step is the sum over the asked rows and columns, divided by the asked rows.

Which sentences. The base learns only from sentences whose own words land (landed, D75). About 2.2–2.6 % of the sentences do not land when their own words are flown, mostly high at the DA. Their last words would teach an end that does not land. Flights with a faulty observed track are left out too (D111). The artefact is not changed: the selection happens when the sentences are read. The excluded flights come back in the post-training as starts of closed loops.

Stop. The training keeps the epoch with the smallest per-step loss on the select days. It never reads the validation days. They are read one time for each stage (D85).

Cross-validation by airport. The goal is a new airport. So a fold trains on the train days of four airports and reads the select days of the fifth. The campaign has 31 runs. The rules are fixed before the runs:

  • The score of a run is the mean, over the 5 folds, of the per-step loss on the held-out airport.
  • The seed scale is the absolute difference between two seeds of configuration A.
  • Configuration: among those within twice the seed scale of the best, take the one with the fewest parameters.
  • Variant: “constants” (with the length and the threshold elevation of each runway) only if it is lower than “full” by more than twice the seed scale.

9.7 A new airport

The prior has no identity of an airport. A new airport needs only data that the five airports also come from: the candidate runways with their threshold, course, TCH, glidepath angle and DA, and the airport elevation (from the FAA CIFP); the FAF and the LPV cone for the masks; the landings in the last 30 minutes (from the airport’s tracks or a live feed); and the dynamics of each aircraft type, which do not depend on the airport. Every fold of the cross-validation flies the full closed loop at its held-out airport. The final test is KAUS.

One limit is stated. Speed words are ground speeds. At a high airport, one indicated airspeed is a larger ground speed (about 8 % at E = 1,650 m, one step). The five airports and KAUS all lie below 200 m.
