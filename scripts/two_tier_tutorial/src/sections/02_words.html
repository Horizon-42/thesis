2 The vocabulary

The vocabulary is the language between the two parts. It has five columns. A row is one line of a sentence, and a row is Δ seconds long. In each row, a column says one word or says nothing. “Nothing” has its own value: unchanged.

A speaker says the columns in this order. A later column sees the words that the earlier columns said in the same row. The first predicted step is the row 16 s after row 0. The rows before it are observed only. At the first predicted step, every column says a value.

The row interval Δ is 2, 4 or 8 s. The user chose Δ = 4 s (D11). A speaker says a row, and the executor hears it, every 4 s. The executor itself runs one cycle every 1 s.

The table above the figure shows the words of the rows that were said. Move the slider. At row 0 all five columns say a word. After that, most cells are empty. The heading column changes most often, because a turn is a series of 5° steps. Orange words are correction words. Section 7 explains them.

2.1 Heading words are relative to the runway

A heading word says: fly track θ and keep it, with θ = course(R) + 5k°. R is the runway in force and k is the class of the word. The heading classes go round the full circle. Class 0 is the course of the runway: the final. Class 36 is the opposite direction: the downwind. Classes 18 and 54 are the two base legs.

There are two reasons for a relative grid (D8). Both were measured on the train split.

  1. The final must be on the grid. The capture corridor allows 2° from the course. The courses of KSTL are 2.3° from the nearest absolute 5° value. With absolute words, no word could hold a KSTL final.
  2. The same manoeuvre gets the same word at all airports. This helps a model to use the data of one airport at another airport.

The executor converts a heading word to an absolute track when it hears the word. It keeps that track until the next heading word. A change of runway does not turn the aircraft.

2.2 Altitude words are heights above the airport

An altitude word says: descend or climb to level T and keep it. T is a geometric height above the airport elevation E (D58). The executor flies the level at T + E above sea level. The grid has 40 levels on three segments. The steps are 60 m up to 1,260 m, 120 m up to 2,700 m, and 450 m up to 5,400 m.

A coarse grid high up is enough: 99.7 % of the level words are under 3,000 m. A fine step high up would only divide the day-to-day spread of an assigned level. The word “no level-off” has no level. It means: descend at the angle in force, and do not stop. It is the word of the final descent.

Why above the airport and not above sea level? A word must mean the same manoeuvre at every airport (principle 4). At a high airport, such as KDEN, the levels of a final approach are far above what the five training airports (1–188 m) used. Above E, the final approach lies in the 60 m segment at every airport. The cost: a round level such as 3,000 ft MSL is a different word at each airport. The second part of the demo shows this.

The blue band is the tolerance ε of a level (D52): half the larger gap to the neighbouring levels, plus 10 m. It is 40 m, 70 m or 235 m. The envelope of a word is a tube around the descent to its level. Principle 6 requires that every containment rate comes with the width of the tube.

2.3 Angle words say how steep

The angle column has seven values: unchanged, level, four descent classes and one climb. The four descent classes come from a k-means fit on tan(angle) of the descent pieces of all train days. Each piece counts with the square of its length (D54). The user chose the values rounded to 0.25° (D56).

Descent 3 is the 3.0° glidepath that 24 of the 25 candidate runways publish. The vocabulary has one climb word. The word has no angle: the angle is a value of the executor. While a go-around is in force, the executor climbs at the steepest steady angle that its thrust permits. This angle is not less than 1.885° and not more than 3°.

2.4 Speed words are steps

A speed word says: hold ground speed V. Controllers assign indicated airspeed, but the data has no airspeed and no wind. So the words use ground speed. The word “unspecified” means that the pilot flies the approach speed of the aircraft type. In a labelled sentence, “unspecified” starts at the capture row.

A change of speed says each grid value on the way (D43). Each speed word is then a step of 5 m/s. The time between the words gives the rate of the change, in the same way that the time between heading words gives the rate of a turn. Why? A target word gives no rate. Take one target word and a fixed rate of the executor. Then, in 31 % of the flights, the flown aircraft moves more than 1 km ahead of the observed aircraft along the path (an estimate on the smoke artefact).

2.5 The runway column and the go-around

The runway column keeps two states: R, the runway in force, and G, the go-around state. The executor uses R for two things only: to convert heading words, and to find the distance for the slow-down to the approach speed. The judge uses R for the landing.

There is no clearance word and no runway lock. The labeller cannot see when a controller gave a clearance, because the data has no radio. So the model says the turns with heading words (D2). While G is false, the model can change the runway at any time (D12).

“Go-around” means abandon this approach (D10). It changes no target of another column (D27). The heading word in force stays. A climb word now climbs at the go-around angle, and “unspecified” holds the airspeed. The judge ignores every threshold crossing while G is true. The time limit of the flight grows by 900 s. A runway word ends G, so one column can say the sequence approach → go-around → approach.
