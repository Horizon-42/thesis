How to Read an RNAV (GPS) Approach Chart

Using KRDU RNAV (GPS) Y RWY 5L (Raleigh-Durham International Airport, Runway 05L) as a complete worked example, this tutorial walks you through every number and symbol on a U.S. instrument approach chart — starting from zero.

Amdt 2, 25FEB21 APP CRS 054° GPA 3.00° LPV DA 598 ft For complete beginners

1Introduction: What Is an Instrument Approach Chart?

Imagine you are flying an airliner. The clouds are low, you can see nothing out the window, and you need to land precisely on the correct runway. In that situation, pilots rely on an Instrument Approach Procedure (IAP) — an officially designed, published set of flight paths and altitude requirements. The approach chart (also called an approach plate) is the "instruction manual" for that procedure.

An approach chart tells the pilot four things:

  • Where to begin the approach and which heading to fly toward the runway;
  • The altitude that must be maintained at each key fix (too low risks terrain; too high means misalignment with the runway);
  • When and along which route to execute a missed approach if landing conditions are not met;
  • The minimum visibility and ceiling required to be allowed to land (the minima).
🛩Key Concept: What Is an RNAV (GPS) Approach?

RNAV (Area Navigation) is a technology that lets an aircraft navigate precisely using satellites (GPS/WAAS) or onboard computers — without relying on ground-based radio beacons. An RNAV (GPS) approach guides the aircraft to the runway entirely via GPS signals, with no ILS system required at the runway end. Combined with the U.S. WAAS (Wide Area Augmentation System), it can deliver vertical guidance comparable to an ILS, allowing a Decision Altitude as low as about 200 ft above the ground.

This tutorial uses KRDU RNAV (GPS) Y RWY 5L as its example, walking you through every block and every number on a complete approach chart. No prior flying experience is needed — just follow along patiently.

3Header — Field by Field

3.1 Approach Name and the Letter "Y"

The chart title is RNAV (GPS) Y RWY 5L.

  • RNAV (GPS): This is an Area Navigation GPS approach that does not rely on ground-based radio aids.
  • Y: The letter suffix is simply a chart-sequence identifier — it does not represent any navigation point or quality level. When multiple RNAV (GPS) charts exist for the same runway, they are lettered from the bottom up: Z, Y, X…. At KRDU Runway 05L, the Z chart is a different approach procedure; the Y chart is this tutorial's chart.
  • RWY 5L: Aligned to Runway 05L (magnetic bearing 054°, left-side runway).
⚠️Common Misconception: Y Is Not a Fix

Beginners often assume Y is the name of a navigation aid or fix. In fact it is purely a distinguishing letter meaning "the second chart for this runway" and has no connection to any point on the flight path.

3.2 APP CRS (Final Approach Course)

APP CRS 054°: The aircraft flies on a magnetic bearing of 054° during the final approach segment — roughly northeast toward the runway. The runway designation "05L" corresponds to 054° (the runway magnetic bearing rounded to the nearest 10°, then the trailing zero dropped).

3.3 Runway Length and Elevations

  • Rwy Ldg 10000 ft: Available landing distance 10,000 ft (approximately 3,048 m).
  • TDZE 384 ft: Touchdown Zone Elevation (TDZE) 384 ft — the elevation of the runway touchdown area above mean sea level; used as the reference for computing HAT (Height Above Touchdown).
  • Apt Elev 435 ft: Airport elevation 435 ft — the highest point of the entire airport above MSL; used as the reference for computing HAA (Height Above Airport).

3.4 WAAS Channel and RNP Information

CH 48813 / W05A: The WAAS channel number and WAAS approach ID, allowing a WAAS-capable flight management system to automatically load the precise approach data. The annotation "RNP APCH-GPS" indicates that this procedure is published to ICAO RNP APCH standards, requiring a minimum navigation accuracy of 0.3 NM (0.1 NM on the final approach segment).

3.6 Missed Approach Text

The missed approach text in the header describes in words what to do if a go-around is required:

🔄Missed Approach Instructions

"Climb to 1000 then climbing left turn to 2200 direct DUHAM and hold."

In plain language: climb to 1,000 ft, then turn left while continuing to climb, level off at 2,200 ft, fly direct to fix DUHAM, and enter a holding pattern there.

3.8 MSA (Minimum Safe Altitude)

MSA 3400 ft, centered on RW05L, within a radius of 25 NM — the emergency obstacle clearance altitude. This is a "safety altitude": if a pilot is uncertain of navigation, climbing to MSA guarantees obstacle clearance anywhere within the 25 NM circle. It is not the altitude used during a normal approach.

4Plan View: The Route from Above

The plan view is a top-down diagram of the entire approach procedure. It shows all fixes, flight tracks, the missed approach route, and surrounding terrain/airspace information.

4.1 The Fix Chain

The fixes on the final approach segment, from farthest to nearest, are:

FixRolePublished AltitudeDistance to NextNotes
OTTOS IAF 5000 ft — Initial Approach Fix; one of the entry points where aircraft transition from the en-route structure to the approach
CHWDR IAF 5000 ft — A second IAF for aircraft arriving from a different direction
BOULE Step-down fix 4000 ft 3.1 NM Intermediate step-down; aircraft descend from 5,000 to 4,000 ft
SCHOO IF 3000 ft 2.7 NM Intermediate Fix; aircraft are now aligned with the runway and continue descending
WEPAS FAF 2200 ft — Final Approach Fix; the glidepath begins here — denoted by the ▼ symbol
RW05L MAPt TDZE 384 ft — Runway threshold = LNAV Missed Approach Point (when no vertical guidance is used)
📍Key Concept: Fix Roles
  • IAF (Initial Approach Fix): The entry gate to the approach. This chart has two IAFs (OTTOS and CHWDR), giving aircraft a convenient starting point regardless of which direction they approach from.
  • IF (Intermediate Fix): SCHOO. By this point the aircraft is aligned with the runway, speed and configuration are stabilizing, and it is ready for the final approach.
  • FAF (Final Approach Fix): WEPAS. The glidepath is established here and the aircraft begins the final 3° descent. Marked on the chart with a black triangle ▼.
  • MAPt (Missed Approach Point): For an LNAV approach (no vertical guidance), the MAPt is at RW05L (the runway threshold). For LPV/LNAV-VNAV approaches, the MAPt is the instant the aircraft descends to the DA — not a ground fix, but an altitude.

4.3 TAA and the OTTOS Holding Pattern

Near OTTOS the chart shows "2T0K" and an altitude circle of 6,000 ft — this is the TAA (Terminal Arrival Area) annotation. The TAA defines the minimum arrival altitude for each sector when joining the approach from different directions, ensuring the aircraft has adequate altitude margin before reaching the IAF. The details are beyond this tutorial's scope; the key takeaway is: when reaching OTTOS, the aircraft must be at or above 6,000 ft.

5Profile View: The Vertical Step-Down

The profile view shows the aircraft's descent from a side perspective — like a staircase, where each step represents an altitude to which descent is authorized. The final approach segment also displays a 3° glidepath line extending from the FAF (WEPAS) to the runway threshold.

Fig. 5-1 · KRDU RNAV Y RWY 5L profile view schematic (altitudes and distances are illustrative, not to scale)

5.1 What Each Step Means

The step-down descent is the classic pattern of an instrument approach:

  • Each step-down fix sets a minimum altitude — an "at or above" constraint.
  • Between two step-down fixes the aircraft holds at that altitude until reaching the next fix, at which point it may descend further.
  • This design ensures that as long as the aircraft has not yet reached the safe approach path, it remains clear of surrounding terrain and obstacles.

5.2 Glidepath Angle and TCH

From WEPAS (2,200 ft) to RW05L (384 ft) there is a 3.00° glidepath. This means the aircraft descends approximately 320 ft for every 1 nautical mile (1,852 m) of forward travel. TCH = 57 ft: the glidepath crosses the runway threshold at a height of 57 ft — meaning the landing gear is approximately 57 ft above the runway surface as the aircraft passes over the threshold.

ℹ️VGSI and RNAV Glidepath Are Not Coincident

A note on the chart reads: "VGSI and RNAV glidepath not coincident (VGSI Angle 3.00/TCH 72)". This means that although the Visual Glideslope Indicator (PAPI/VASI) and the RNAV glidepath share the same 3° angle, their threshold crossing heights differ — PAPI at 72 ft, RNAV at 57 ft. Pilots should not rely solely on the PAPI to judge whether they are on the RNAV glidepath during final approach.

6The Four Approach Segments

The FAA divides an instrument approach from start to touchdown into four standard segments:

SegmentNameStarts atEnds atOn this chartPurpose
Initial Approach Segment Initial Approach Segment IAF IF or FAF OTTOS/CHWDR → SCHOO Aircraft lose altitude and turn to align with the runway
Intermediate Approach Segment Intermediate Approach Segment IF (SCHOO) FAF (WEPAS) SCHOO → WEPAS Stabilize configuration and speed in preparation for final approach
Final Approach Segment Final Approach Segment FAF (WEPAS) MAPt / runway threshold WEPAS → RW05L Descend on the 3° glidepath and decide whether to land
Missed Approach Segment Missed Approach Segment MAPt MAHF (DUHAM) RW05L → DUHAM If visual contact with the runway is not established, climb and enter the hold
✅Memory Aid

Think of the four segments as Initial → Intermediate → Final → Missed, with key fixes IAF → IF → FAF → MAPt → MAHF. Each segment has a different pilot focus: Initial = lose altitude; Intermediate = stabilize; Final = landing decision; Missed = safe departure.

7The Minima Box

The Minima Box is the table at the bottom of the approach chart. It tells the pilot how low the aircraft may descend given prevailing weather conditions. If the pilot cannot see the runway upon reaching the Decision Altitude (DA) or Minimum Descent Altitude (MDA), a missed approach must be executed immediately — further descent is not permitted.

7.2 DA vs. MDA

TypeFull NameWhen UsedOperating Rule
DA Decision Altitude With vertical guidance (LPV / LNAV-VNAV) At this altitude the pilot decides: runway in sight → continue; otherwise → immediately go missed
MDA Minimum Descent Altitude No vertical guidance (LNAV) Level off at MDA and fly level while looking for the runway visually; go missed at the MAPt if runway not in sight

7.3 HAT and HAA

  • HAT (Height Above Touchdown): The minima altitude minus TDZE (384 ft). For example, LPV DA 598 − 384 = 214 ft HAT — meaning the pilot must see the runway at 214 ft above the touchdown zone.
  • HAA (Height Above Airport): Used for circling approaches only — minima altitude minus airport elevation (435 ft). Because the aircraft is not following the approach centerline during circling, the reference is the overall airport elevation.

7.4 Why Is the LPV Minimum the Lowest?

Ranked from lowest to highest DA/MDA (most to least capable):

  1. LPV (lowest, DA 598 ft): Requires a WAAS receiver and certified aircraft. Provides precise lateral and vertical guidance (similar to an ILS), allowing the pilot to follow a precise 3° glidepath to a DA of 598 ft.
  2. LNAV/VNAV (middle, DA 748 ft): Requires barometric vertical navigation (Baro-VNAV) or SBAS. Provides vertical guidance but with less precision than LPV, hence the higher DA. May be unavailable at extreme temperatures (see briefing notes in the header).
  3. LNAV (highest, MDA 840 ft): Lateral navigation only — no vertical guidance. The pilot descends by step-down fixes to the MDA and then levels off, visually searching for the runway. The allowed altitude is therefore higher and visibility requirements are expressed in statute miles rather than RVR.
💡Pilots Prefer LPV

When the aircraft is WAAS-capable and the flight plan authorizes LPV, pilots should use LPV minima whenever possible — it offers the most precise guidance, the lowest DA, and the greatest chance of landing in poor weather (low ceilings, low visibility).

7.5 Circling Approach

A circling approach is a special case: the aircraft executes an instrument approach aligned with one runway, then maneuvers visually to land on a different runway. Because the aircraft is no longer following the approach centerline, greater altitude margin is required, so the MDA is higher (960–1,180 ft) and varies by aircraft category (Cat A/B/C/D) — faster aircraft have larger turn radii and therefore need more protection altitude.

8.2 Altitude Constraints at Each Fix on This Chart

On the KRDU RNAV Y RWY 5L chart, every step-down fix uses an "at or above" constraint — meaning the aircraft must be at the published altitude or higher when it reaches that fix. In CIFP (the FAA's machine-readable Coded Instrument Flight Procedures data), these step-down fixes are encoded with an "at or above" qualifier.

BOULE4000 ftAt or aboveMust not be below 4,000 ft when crossing BOULE

🚫Never Do This

Never descend below the published altitude before reaching the associated fix. For example, while between SCHOO (IF) and WEPAS (FAF), descending below 2,200 ft is not permitted — even though the FAF altitude is 2,200 ft, the aircraft must first reach WEPAS before beginning that descent. Premature descent means the aircraft may leave the obstacle clearance envelope, creating a collision risk with terrain or structures.

8.3 How to Identify the Symbols on the Chart

On the actual chart, an "at or above" altitude has a thin underline below the number; a "mandatory" altitude places the number inside a thin rectangle. Recognizing these two symbols is a fundamental skill for reading approach charts.

9A Glimpse at CIFP Leg Coding

The paper chart is designed for human pilots; but aircraft Flight Management Systems (FMS) and analysis tools like AeroViz need a machine-readable version. The FAA publishes CIFP (Coded Instrument Flight Procedures) in the ARINC 424 format, encoding each segment of the chart as a row of fixed-width text records.

9.1 Path and Terminator (P-T) Codes

Each leg is described by a two-letter code (Path and Terminator, P-T) that describes its geometry:

Code
Full Name and Meaning
IF
Initial Fix: Declares a starting point; no flight action is taken. The first leg of every procedure is usually an IF leg.
TF
Track to a Fix: Fly a great-circle track to the next fix. This is the most common encoding for straight segments; most legs on this chart are TF.
CF
Course to a Fix: Fly a specified magnetic course to the fix (the course is fixed and does not follow the great circle). The final approach segment typically uses CF to ensure precise alignment on 054°.
DF
Direct to a Fix: Fly direct from the current position to the fix, with no specified course. Commonly used after a missed approach turn to fly direct to DUHAM.
CA
Course to an Altitude: Fly a specified heading until reaching a specified altitude. The initial climb segment of the missed approach (climb to 1,000 ft) is a CA leg.
HM
Hold to Manual termination: Enter a standard holding pattern at a fix and hold until the controller clears the aircraft out or the pilot manually exits. The DUHAM hold is an HM leg.

9.2 Approximate Coding Logic for This Chart

💻CIFP Coding Illustration

The logic of the final approach segment is roughly as follows (simplified example — not actual CIFP record lines):

  • IF OTTOS 5000A (A = at or above; start at OTTOS, not below 5,000)
  • TF BOULE 4000A (track to BOULE, not below 4,000)
  • TF SCHOO 3000A (continue to SCHOO, not below 3,000)
  • TF WEPAS 2200A (to FAF WEPAS, not below 2,200; glidepath begins)
  • CF RW05L 054° (fly course 054° to the runway threshold — the final approach segment)
  • CA — 1000 (missed approach: climb on current course to 1,000 ft)
  • DF DUHAM 2200 (fly direct to DUHAM, climb to 2,200 ft)
  • HM DUHAM — (enter the hold at DUHAM until cleared by ATC)
