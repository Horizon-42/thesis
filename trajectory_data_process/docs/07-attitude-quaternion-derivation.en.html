trajectory_data_process / Stage 3 · CZML orientation

Attitude → Quaternion: how a trajectory learns which way its nose points

The CZML position property says where an aircraft is. The orientation property says which way it points — and unlike position, it is nowhere in the data. This page derives it from scratch, the way generate_czml.py does: velocity → heading + pitch, then two frame rotations into a single ECEF unit quaternion. Every step has a 3-D widget you can drive.

1 / The missing field

Position is measured; attitude has to be invented

By the time a flight reaches Stage 3, each waypoint is just [offset_sec, lon, lat, alt] — four numbers, no heading, no pitch, no roll. The raw ADS-B track angle existed back in Stage 1 but was deliberately dropped at the interchange seam. So before Cesium can point a glTF model down the flight path, Stage 3 must reconstruct the full 3-D nose direction from the geometry of the path itself.

The reconstruction rests on one physical idea and one admission:

Attitude angleRecoverable?How
heading (yaw)✅ yesdirection of travel to the next waypoint
pitch✅ yesclimb/descent angle of that same segment
roll (bank)❌ nono bank information in position data → assumed 0
The plan. Two steps. Step 1 turns the velocity vector between consecutive waypoints into a (heading, pitch) pair. Step 2 turns that pair — at a given (lon, lat) — into the ECEF unit quaternion CZML wants. Step 2 is itself two nested rotations: model → ENU then ENU → ECEF.
2 / Frames & quaternions

What the quaternion actually encodes

Cesium renders everything in ECEF — Earth-Centered, Earth-Fixed: the origin sits at the planet's centre and the axes are bolted to the rotating Earth. An aircraft's orientation is the single rotation that carries the model's own body axes onto ECEF axes. That rotation is stored as a unit quaternion \((x,y,z,w)\).

Three frames appear throughout this page. Keep them straight and the rest is bookkeeping:

FrameAxesDepends on
Model / body +X nose +Y left +Z up the glTF file
ENU (local tangent)X = East, Y = North, Z = Upthe position (lon, lat)
ECEF (world)fixed to the planetnothing — it is the global frame

Why a quaternion and not three angles?

No gimbal lockEuler angles degenerate as pitch → ±90°; a quaternion never does.
Clean interpolationBetween samples Cesium does SLERP — a constant-speed, shortest-arc blend — smooth even through steep turns.
Native CZML typeorientation.unitQuaternion is exactly this 4-number rotation, one sample per waypoint.
3 / Step 1 — velocity → heading + pitch

Reading the flight path as a velocity vector

compute_velocity_orientation() walks the waypoints. For each one it builds the segment to the next waypoint (a forward difference); the final waypoint has no "next", so it reuses the previous segment (a backward difference) — that keeps the parked model pointing sensibly at the end of playback instead of snapping to a default heading.

From a segment \(P_1=(\lambda_1,\varphi_1,h_1)\) to \(P_2=(\lambda_2,\varphi_2,h_2)\):

Heading is the initial great-circle bearing (geokit bearing_rad) — \(0=\text{North}\), increasing clockwise:

\[ \theta_{\text{hdg}}=\operatorname{atan2}\!\Big(\sin\Delta\lambda\,\cos\varphi_2,\;\; \cos\varphi_1\sin\varphi_2-\sin\varphi_1\cos\varphi_2\cos\Delta\lambda\Big),\qquad \Delta\lambda=\lambda_2-\lambda_1 \]

Pitch is the climb angle of the same segment — the vertical rise over the horizontal run \(d\) (geokit haversine_m, the great-circle ground distance):

\[ \theta_{\text{pitch}}=\operatorname{atan2}\big(h_2-h_1,\;\; d\big),\qquad d=\text{haversine}(P_1,P_2) \]

Roll \(=0\). And the degenerate cases are explicit: an empty track yields no samples; a single lonely waypoint yields \((\text{heading}=0,\ \text{pitch}=0)\).

4 / Step 2a — model → ENU

Placing the aircraft in the local horizon

The recovered \((heading, pitch)\) are angles in the aircraft's local horizon. The first rotation, m_model, expresses the model's three body axes as vectors in the ENU frame. The trick to reading it: each column is where one body axis goes.

\[ R_{\text{model}\to\text{ENU}}= \begin{bmatrix} \sin h\cos\theta & -\cos h & -\sin h\sin\theta \\ \cos h\cos\theta & \;\;\sin h & -\cos h\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & 0 & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} \quad{\scriptstyle(h=\text{heading},\ \theta=\text{pitch})} \]
ColumnBody axisAs an ENU vector \((E,N,U)\)Meaning
0+X nose\((\sin h\cos\theta,\ \cos h\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)\)the velocity direction: bearing \(h\), tilted up by \(\theta\)
1+Y left\((-\cos h,\ \sin h,\ 0)\)horizontal, 90° to port — no \(\theta\) term because roll = 0 keeps the wings level
2+Z up\((-\sin h\sin\theta,\ -\cos h\sin\theta,\ \cos\theta)\)body-up, tilted back by the pitch; equals ENU "up" when \(\theta=0\)

5 / Step 2b — ENU → ECEF

The local horizon is itself tilted on the globe

"Up" in Raleigh is not "up" in London. The local ENU frame is tilted relative to the planet's fixed axes by where you stand on Earth. The second rotation, m_enu, captures that — at longitude \(\lambda\) and latitude \(\varphi\), its columns are the East, North and Up directions written in ECEF:

\[ R_{\text{ENU}\to\text{ECEF}}= \begin{bmatrix} -\sin\lambda & -\sin\varphi\cos\lambda & \cos\varphi\cos\lambda \\ \;\;\cos\lambda & -\sin\varphi\sin\lambda & \cos\varphi\sin\lambda \\ 0 & \cos\varphi & \sin\varphi \end{bmatrix} \]

The third column \((\cos\varphi\cos\lambda,\ \cos\varphi\sin\lambda,\ \sin\varphi)\) is just the outward radial unit vector — "Up" always points straight away from the Earth's centre. The other two columns are the tangent directions. You can watch this matrix swing around as you fly the aircraft across the globe in the full sandbox below.

6 / Compose → quaternion

Two rotations, then a stable matrix → quaternion conversion

Apply model→ENU first, then ENU→ECEF. Matrix multiplication composes them right-to-left:

\[ R_{\text{model}\to\text{ECEF}} \;=\; R_{\text{ENU}\to\text{ECEF}}\; R_{\text{model}\to\text{ENU}} \;=\; \texttt{m\_enu} \cdot \texttt{m\_model} \]

That 3×3 rotation is finally turned into the unit quaternion CZML stores, using Shepperd's method (_mat3_to_quaternion). The naïve formula \(w=\tfrac12\sqrt{1+\operatorname{tr}R}\) divides by a quantity that collapses to zero near a 180° rotation; Shepperd's method instead picks whichever of the four pivots \(\{\operatorname{tr}R,\;R_{00},\;R_{11},\;R_{22}\}\) is largest, so the divisor is always safely away from zero: 

# the trace branch (largest pivot = trace); three symmetric branches handle the rest
if trace > 0:
    s = 0.5 / sqrt(trace + 1.0)
    w = 0.25 / s
    x = (m[2][1] - m[1][2]) * s
    y = (m[0][2] - m[2][0]) * s
    z = (m[1][0] - m[0][1]) * s

The result \((x,y,z,w)\) is written once per waypoint into the flat unitQuaternion array. The sandbox uses this exact quaternion to orient its aircraft — so if the plane points down its velocity arrow there, the whole chain is verified end to end. 

# generate_czml.py — the whole pipeline in four lines
heading, pitch = compute_velocity_orientation(waypoints)[i]   # step 1
m_model        = model_to_enu(heading, pitch)                  # step 2a
m_ecef         = mat3_multiply(enu_to_ecef(lon, lat), m_model) # step 2b + compose
x, y, z, w     = mat3_to_quaternion(m_ecef)                  # Shepperd

8 / Caveats

What the derivation cannot know

LimitationConsequence
Roll = 0The model never visibly banks into a turn — it yaws wings-level. There is simply no bank signal in position-only data.
Finite differencesHeading and pitch come from consecutive samples, so noisy or sparse tracks give a jittery nose. Cesium's SLERP smooths the playback, not the source angles.
Last waypointIt has no successor, so it inherits the previous segment's direction — a parked aircraft keeps its final heading rather than snapping to north.
Stationary pointsIf two samples share a position, \(d\to0\) and pitch is forced to 0 to avoid an undefined climb angle.
Why it is "computed, not carried." Heading existed in Stage 1 as track; pitch and roll were never measured. Rather than thread an aviation heading through the neutral interchange format, the pipeline keeps Stage 2 attitude-free and re-derives the full 3-D nose direction at the only stage that needs it — keeping the seam simple at the cost of the wings-level approximation.
